Scoutr's core value proposition is a profit estimate. Paste a product URL, get back a clear picture of what each product on that page is worth as an Amazon or eBay resell opportunity.

That profit estimate is only worth anything if the fee calculation is accurate. An off-by-50% margin estimate sends resellers toward products that lose money. Getting the fees right is not a nice-to-have — it's the entire basis of the product's usefulness.

## The complexity of Amazon fees

Amazon FBA fees are more complex than they appear from the outside. The headline number — "15% referral fee" — is accurate for some categories. For others, it's wrong by a significant margin.

The full fee structure for a typical Amazon FBA sale includes:

**Referral fees** — a percentage of the sale price, varying by category. Electronics are 8%. Beauty products are 8%. Jewellery is 20%. Books are 15%. The category matters enormously for margin calculation.

**Fulfillment fees** — charged per unit, based on size and weight. The Amazon fee schedule has six size tiers: small standard, large standard, small oversize, medium oversize, large oversize, and special oversize. A product that's 1.1 lbs triggers a different tier than one at 0.9 lbs, with meaningful fee differences.

**Monthly storage fees** — charged per cubic foot, varying by month. January-September storage is cheaper than October-December. For a reseller turning inventory quickly, storage fees are often negligible. For slow-moving stock, they're material.

**Closing fees** — certain categories (books, music, video, software) have per-item closing fees in addition to referral fees.

The right fee for a product depends on: its category, its dimensions, its weight, what month it's likely to sell in, and whether it qualifies for any fee exceptions. Getting all of that right from a product page URL is a data and inference problem.

## How we solve it

Scoutr's approach uses Claude for the parts that require judgment and structured lookups for the parts that are deterministic.

When a user pastes a URL, Scoutr extracts the product data from the page. For each product, Claude is asked to:
1. Classify the product into an Amazon category
2. Estimate dimensions and weight from available data (model number, product description, comparative products)
3. Flag any known fee exceptions that apply

With category, estimated dimensions, and estimated weight, the fee calculation becomes deterministic. We maintain a current version of Amazon's fee schedule as structured data and apply it programmatically.

The referral fee calculation is straightforward once you have the category. The fulfillment fee calculation requires mapping dimensions and weight to a size tier, then looking up the tier fee. This is pure arithmetic once the data is right.

## The weight and dimension problem

The hardest part is getting weight and dimensions. Product pages don't always list them. When they do, the format is inconsistent. When they don't, you're estimating.

Claude is reasonably good at estimating product dimensions from context. A product listed as "6-pack of paper towel rolls" can have its approximate dimensions estimated from knowledge of standard paper towel roll sizing. A product with a model number that matches a known product family can inherit dimensions from the known product.

But estimates are estimates. Scoutr is explicit about this — the fee breakdown shows when dimensions were estimated rather than extracted, and the profit figure flags uncertainty when the calculation depended on estimated inputs.

We'd rather be right about uncertainty than confident about the wrong number.

## The eBay calculation

eBay's fee structure is simpler than Amazon's but involves its own complications:

**Final value fees** — 13.25% for most categories, capped at a maximum that varies by category. Below the cap, this is straightforward. Above the cap, the fee is fixed.

**Payment processing** — eBay's managed payments take a percentage of the total including shipping. This is often overlooked and understates costs by several percent.

**Insertion fees** — free for your first N listings per month, then a small per-listing fee above the allowance.

**Shipping** — eBay buyers often expect free shipping, which means shipping cost comes out of the seller's margin. We estimate shipping cost from product dimensions and weight against standard carrier rates.

The eBay calculation is less complex to implement correctly but still requires accurate product data for the fee logic to produce useful output.

## Validation approach

We validated the fee calculator against our own test purchases — buying products, listing them, and comparing what Scoutr predicted against what we actually paid. For most products, the difference was under 5%. For a few products with unusual category fees or oversized dimensions, it was higher until we corrected the underlying logic.

The test purchase validation process also revealed that some product categories have minimum referral fees that apply even when the percentage-based fee would be lower. This edge case was in the fee schedule but easy to miss — we caught it through testing.

## The ongoing maintenance

Amazon and eBay update their fee schedules periodically. The fee data that's current today may be slightly off in six months.

Scoutr treats fee data as a versioned dataset. When Amazon announces fee changes (they typically give notice), we update the fee schedule and verify that the calculations still validate against expected outputs. This is a maintenance task, not a build task — but it's a real one.

The practical implication: Scoutr's margin estimates are accurate for current fee structures. We don't guarantee they reflect future fee changes. We update when changes are announced.

## What this makes possible

The margin estimate Scoutr produces isn't a rough approximation. For most products, it's within a few percent of what a seller would actually net. That's accurate enough to make sourcing decisions on.

That accuracy is what makes the product useful. A tool that says "this might be profitable" is marginally useful. A tool that says "this is 34% margin after all fees, calculated from these inputs, with these caveats" is a tool you can make decisions from.

The technical complexity is invisible to the user. They paste a URL and see numbers. Behind those numbers is category classification, dimension estimation, fee schedule lookup, shipping estimation, and explicit handling of uncertainty. That's the product.
