A user complaint is a gift. It tells you something specific that doesn't work, from someone who cared enough to tell you about it. Most users who encounter a problem don't report it — they leave.

When a complaint comes in, the question is: what do you do with it?

## The intake

We receive user feedback through three channels: the in-product contact form, direct email, and the support email address listed in product footers.

The first step is triage. What kind of feedback is this?

**Broken functionality**: something that used to work no longer works, or something that should work doesn't. This is highest priority. A broken feature means users can't do something they're paying for.

**Wrong output**: the product works as designed but produces incorrect results. For Scoutr: a product miscategorised, leading to a wrong fee estimate. For The Hyve: the AI agent giving advice that contradicts the project's documented decisions. This is high priority — users may be making decisions based on wrong information.

**Confusing UX**: the product works correctly but the user couldn't figure out how to do something, or misunderstood what the output meant. This is medium priority — the product isn't broken but the experience is causing friction.

**Feature request**: the user wants something the product doesn't do. Lower priority than fixing what exists, but valuable signal about what to build next.

## The response

Every complaint gets a response within 24 hours. Not "we've received your feedback and will look into it" — a substantive response that acknowledges what the user reported and tells them what we're going to do about it.

For broken functionality or wrong output, the response confirms that we've reproduced the issue and tells the user our timeline for the fix. If we can fix it same day, we say so. If it requires more investigation, we give an honest estimate.

For confusing UX, the response often includes an explanation of what the product is doing and why — the fix for confusing UX isn't always changing the product; sometimes it's helping the user understand the product.

For feature requests, we're honest about whether it's on the roadmap. "We're actively considering this" when we are, "this isn't in our current plans but we'll keep it in mind" when it isn't.

## The investigation

For broken functionality and wrong output, the investigation follows the debugging process we've written about separately. The additional constraint when a user has reported it: you want to work quickly without creating a sense of urgency that leads to a sloppy fix.

The investigation steps:
1. Reproduce with the information provided (or ask for more information if needed)
2. Characterise the failure type
3. Identify whether this is a known issue or a new one
4. Assess the scope: how many users could be affected? Is this a systematic failure or an edge case?

The scope assessment matters for prioritisation. An edge case that one user encountered is different from a systematic failure that affects 20% of scans. Both get fixed, but not necessarily in the same order.

## The fix

Once the investigation produces a diagnosis, the fix can usually be categorised:

**Quick fix**: a prompt change, a bug in a specific function, a data issue. These ship within hours. We fix, verify, deploy, and follow up with the user.

**Structural fix**: an architectural issue, a data model problem, a design decision that needs reconsidering. These take longer. We ship a short-term fix (mitigating the immediate problem), communicate with the user about the timeline for the complete fix, and schedule the structural work.

**Known limitation**: the product does exactly what it was designed to do, but what it does isn't sufficient for this user's needs. We communicate honestly about whether this is something that will change and whether the product is a good fit for the user's specific use case.

## The follow-up

After a fix ships, we follow up with the user who reported the issue. "The fix for this shipped in today's build — you should see the correct behaviour now." Most users are surprised by this. They reported a problem and assumed it would be ignored or take weeks to address.

The follow-up serves two purposes: it confirms the fix is working for the user who reported it (sometimes the fix works generally but not for the specific case), and it builds trust that feedback is acted on.

Users who feel heard come back. Users who feel ignored churn. The follow-up is cheap; the trust it builds is not.

## What we've learned

**The complaint that matters most is the one you didn't get.** For every user who reports a problem, several encountered it silently. When a user reports something, assume the problem is more widespread than the single report suggests.

**The fastest path to a fix is often asking the user.** "Can you send me the URL you were scanning when this happened?" gets you the exact reproduction case faster than any other approach. Users want to help you fix their problem.

**Document everything.** A fixed bug that isn't documented is a bug that will be reintroduced. Every fix goes in the commit message with context, and significant fixes get decision log entries explaining the root cause.

**Don't over-apologise.** A genuine apology for something that was broken is appropriate. Excessive apology for every problem creates an expectation of perfection that isn't helpful. Acknowledge the issue, fix it, confirm the fix. That's the right ratio.
