Most SEO advice assumes you are a content site. Produce 500 pieces of keyword-targeted content, build backlinks, optimise metadata, and watch traffic grow. The advice is not wrong for that context — it's just irrelevant for SaaS products that are tools, not publications.

Scoutr, The Marqet, and The Hyve are tools. The journal aside, they don't produce content. Here's how we approach search for product-first businesses.

## What actually drives SaaS discovery

A tool gets discovered through:

1. **Word of mouth:** A user recommends it to someone with the same problem. High conversion rate (person already has context), hard to engineer.
2. **Comparison searches:** Someone looking at "alternatives to [existing tool]" or "[tool category] comparison". High intent, competitive.
3. **Problem searches:** Someone with a specific problem searching for a solution. Often long-tail, often lower competition.
4. **Brand searches:** Someone who heard about the product searching directly for it. Zero competition, indicates brand recognition.
5. **Content discovery:** Technical content that attracts people who then discover the product. This is what the journal does for Construx.

Standard SEO advice focuses on #3 and tries to scale it through volume. For a content site that works. For a SaaS tool, the better leverage is often #5 — producing genuinely useful technical content that attracts the right audience.

## What we do for SEO

**Technical foundations:** Every page has a proper `<title>`, `<meta name="description">`, and canonical URL. Open Graph tags are set so link previews work correctly on Twitter/X, Slack, and iMessage. This is table stakes — not a strategy.

```tsx
// app/layout.tsx
export const metadata: Metadata = {
  title: {
    template: '%s | Construx Group',
    default: 'Construx Group — Build, Ship, Grow',
  },
  description: 'Three SaaS products built in public...',
  openGraph: {
    type: 'website',
    siteName: 'Construx Group',
  },
};
```

**Structured data for journal content:** Journal dispatches have `Article` schema markup so search engines understand they're editorial content, not marketing pages.

**Sitemap:** Auto-generated from `app/sitemap.ts`, listing all static pages and all journal dispatches. Submitted to Google Search Console.

**Page speed:** Fast pages rank better and convert better. We use Next.js static generation wherever possible, Tailwind CSS (no runtime CSS-in-JS), and serve assets from CloudFront CDN. Core Web Vitals are consistently good.

**The journal itself:** 209 dispatches about real problems, with real code, covering real topics. This produces long-tail search traffic over time. A dispatch about PgBouncer connection pooling will be found by someone searching "serverless postgres too many connections" for years after it's published.

## What we don't do

**Keyword research-driven content creation.** We don't write dispatches to target keywords. We write dispatches about decisions we're actually making. The topics are determined by what we're building, not by what has search volume.

This is a deliberate choice with a cost: we miss topics that have high search volume but aren't directly relevant to our current work. The benefit is that the content is authentic — it describes what we're actually doing, which makes it more credible and more useful to the people who find it.

**Link building.** We don't do outreach to get backlinks. Links happen organically (when someone finds a dispatch useful and links to it) or not at all. This is slower than an active link building strategy and produces fewer links. We've decided the trade-off is worth it: our time is better spent building the products.

**Programmatic SEO.** Generating thousands of thin pages targeting location-specific or feature-specific keywords. This works for certain business models (job boards, directory sites, real estate listings). It's not relevant for our tools.

**Social media SEO signals.** We have minimal social media presence. Social signals are a minor ranking factor at best.

## The honest numbers

The journal generates consistent organic traffic. Individual dispatches rank for specific long-tail queries. The compounding effect of 200+ dispatches means total organic traffic grows steadily without new effort.

The product pages (Scoutr, The Marqet, The Hyve) get minimal organic traffic. People searching for "dropshipping product research tool" or "team communication software" land on competitors with established domain authority. We're not competitive on those head terms.

The realistic path to product SEO for a new SaaS is: produce useful content in your domain, build domain authority over 12-24 months, then start competing on product-adjacent queries. We're in that 12-24 month period.

## What matters more than SEO

For early-stage SaaS, SEO is often the wrong priority. A tool with 50 active users who genuinely value it is in better shape than a tool with 5,000 monthly visitors who don't convert.

The priorities for a new SaaS:
1. **Product quality:** The tool works, is fast, and solves the problem. No amount of SEO rescues a product people don't want to use.
2. **Retention:** People who try the tool keep using it. This is the prerequisite for word-of-mouth growth.
3. **Narrow distribution:** Find the 10 people in the world with exactly your problem and talk to them directly. Solve their problem well. They'll find the next 10.
4. **SEO:** A long-game investment that compounds over years.

We invest in SEO through the journal because the cost is low — we write dispatches anyway, and good technical content has SEO value as a side effect. We don't run an SEO program. We build products and document what we build.

## The only SEO metric that matters

Whether organic visitors convert to users.

Traffic that doesn't convert is vanity. A thousand visitors who read one dispatch and leave haven't moved the needle. We track organic traffic in Google Search Console but we weight it much less heavily than user activation rates.

The journal's job is not to drive page views. It's to attract people who want to use or work with the products. Everything else is secondary.
