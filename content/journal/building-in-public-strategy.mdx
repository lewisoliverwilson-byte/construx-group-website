"Building in public" has been absorbed into the startup content playbook. It means, in practice, a Twitter/X account that posts metrics milestones, subscriber counts, and revenue updates. The format is: "Day 47: $1,200 MRR. Thread on what's working."

This is one version of building in public. It's not the version we practice.

## What we think building in public actually means

Building in public means making your thinking visible — not just your outcomes. The outcome post ("we hit $10k MRR") is the summary. The process post ("here's why our pricing was wrong and how we figured it out") is the actual building in public.

Sharing outcomes is easy and doesn't require vulnerability. The outcome is already known, fixed, and defensible. Sharing process requires admitting uncertainty, exposing reasoning that might be wrong, and committing positions before they've been validated.

Process posts are more useful to readers because they transfer method, not just results. A reader who sees "$10k MRR" learns that you can make $10k MRR. A reader who understands your pricing iteration learns how to think about pricing.

The journal is process-heavy for this reason. We write about how we decided, what we considered and rejected, what we got wrong and changed, not primarily what we achieved.

## Authenticity vs. polish

There's a tension between writing that's useful and writing that's polished. Polished writing takes time and produces content that reads well; useful writing can be rougher but captures the actual thinking.

We've landed on a middle position: the posts are coherent and edited, but they're written from the actual perspective of what we're doing, not retrospectively from a position of having figured it out. Posts are written during the process, not after.

This means some posts will age poorly. A post written about Scoutr's pricing while we're in the middle of figuring it out will, if we continue to iterate, be superseded by later understanding. We leave the earlier post. The record of thinking-in-progress is more honest than a retrospective "here's what we should have done."

## What we don't share

Building in public has natural limits. Things we don't publish:

**Active negotiations and legal matters.** Anything that could compromise an active commercial relationship or create legal liability.

**Personal information about team members.** The journal uses "we" and occasionally "Lewis Wilson" — it doesn't expose personal details about the people building.

**Specific financial data beyond rounded numbers.** The journal doesn't include revenue figures, subscriber counts, or cost specifics. "We raised pricing from £9 to £35" yes; "monthly recurring revenue is £X" no.

**Unprocessed real-time information.** We don't post about crises as they're happening. The incident response post was written after the incidents, not during.

These limits aren't embarrassment — they're appropriate boundaries for a publication that's attached to operating businesses.

## The compound return

The most useful thing we can say about the journal after 89 dispatches: the posts that compound most are the specific, process-heavy ones.

The broad strategic posts ("here's how we think about product-market fit") find a general audience. The specific technical and operational posts ("here's how we implemented pgvector search for The Marqet") find the specific audience that will actually use that information.

The specific audience is smaller, but their engagement is higher and their conversion to product users is higher. "Building in public" done with specificity is a product channel. Done with generality, it's just content.

## Why we do it

The honest answer: we do it because writing clarifies thinking. The discipline of writing a coherent post about a decision we've made — the rationale, the trade-offs, the alternatives we rejected — forces precision that the decision-making process itself didn't require.

The external benefit (audience, discovery, product channel) is real and meaningful. The internal benefit (clearer thinking, better decisions) is underrated and might be more important.

Both are sufficient reasons. The combination makes the journal a habit worth maintaining.
