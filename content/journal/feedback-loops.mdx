"Talk to your users" is the most cited advice in startup culture and the most unevenly practiced. Everyone agrees it's important; few teams do it consistently, especially when there are only two people and three products to run.

Here's our actual practice — what we do, how often, and what we've learned.

## The lightweight interview

We do one user interview per week, across all three products. One interview, 30 minutes, via video call or voice. The user is always someone who's actively using the product — not a prospective user, not an evaluator.

We find interviewees through:
- Direct emails to active users ("Would you be up for a 30-minute call about how Scoutr fits into your workflow?")
- The contact form on each product ("If you're open to feedback sessions, include 'feedback' in your message")
- Users who've sent unusually detailed feedback emails

The acceptance rate on direct email outreach is approximately 35%. Most users who've had a positive experience with a product will give you 30 minutes if you ask nicely.

The interview structure:
1. **Context (5 minutes):** Tell me about your eBay selling setup / professional context. Not the product — their world.
2. **Current workflow (10 minutes):** How do you currently solve [the problem the product solves]? Before you used [product], what did you do?
3. **Product usage (10 minutes):** Walk me through how you used [product] last week. What went well? What was frustrating?
4. **Open (5 minutes):** Is there anything the product doesn't do that you wish it did?

We don't ask "how would you rate the product" or "what would you change?" These questions produce generic answers. Asking about actual usage produces specific insight.

## The in-product feedback mechanism

Each product has a small feedback button (a persistent "?" icon) that opens a 3-field form:
1. What were you trying to do?
2. What happened?
3. (Optional) Contact email if you want a response.

The form is low friction by design. No star ratings, no required fields beyond the first two. The goal is capturing the moment of frustration or confusion, not a comprehensive product review.

We receive 10-20 feedback form submissions per week across all three products. The most common category: confusion about how to interpret output (Scoutr's confidence score, The Marqet's listing quality score). The second most common: feature requests.

## The support ticket signal

As mentioned in the support post, every category of support ticket is tracked. Categories with three or more tickets in a week get investigated as potential product clarity problems.

Support tickets are particularly valuable because they represent friction that was bad enough to make a user take action. They're a high-signal subset of all user frustrations.

## The NPS alternative

We don't use NPS (Net Promoter Score). The methodology produces a single number that doesn't tell you why users feel the way they do or what to change.

Instead, we use a quarterly "temperature check" — a simple 4-question email to active users:
1. How often do you use [product]? (daily / weekly / monthly / rarely)
2. What do you use it for most? (open text)
3. What's working well? (open text)
4. What's not working? (open text)

The open text responses are more useful than any numeric score. We read every response.

Response rate: 18-25%, depending on the product and the user segment. This is higher than typical NPS response rates.

## The churn interview

When a paid user cancels, we email them within 24 hours:

> "Sorry to see you go. Could you tell us why you cancelled? Knowing what we could have done better matters a lot to us. Even one sentence helps."

Churn interview response rate: approximately 40%. The responses are often candid in ways that in-use feedback isn't — the user has already decided to leave, so there's nothing to soften.

Categories of churn reasons, across all products:
- "Too expensive for my usage volume" — 35%
- "Found an alternative" — 25%
- "Feature I needed wasn't available" — 20%
- "Wasn't using it enough to justify the cost" — 15%
- "Didn't understand how to get value" — 5%

The 5% who didn't understand how to get value is the most actionable: these are users we could have retained with better onboarding. The 35% who found it too expensive is the most complex: some of these users were genuinely underserved by the pricing, others were underserved by the product.

## The compounding feedback

The most valuable output of consistent user feedback isn't any individual insight — it's the pattern over time. A single user mentioning that the confidence score is confusing is interesting. Twelve users mentioning it across six months is a product problem that requires a design solution.

We keep a running log of product insights by theme. Each interview and support analysis adds to it. When a theme reaches five independent mentions, it goes on the product roadmap.

The pattern recognition is the real output of the practice. Individual data points are noise; repeated patterns are signal. This is why consistency matters more than any single elaborate research method — one interview per week for 52 weeks gives you 52 data points and the ability to see patterns that one big research sprint never would.
