Product roadmaps are a form of commitment. You're committing to build specific things in a specific sequence over a specific timeframe. Teams find this useful for coordination, investors find it useful for evaluation, and customers find it useful for planning.

For a two-person AI company building at the current pace of change, a traditional roadmap is a fiction that creates false precision and real constraint.

## What's wrong with roadmaps at our stage

A roadmap is built on assumptions about the future: what users will need, what the technology will support, what the market will look like in six months. At our stage, those assumptions are mostly wrong.

In the six months since launch, several things have changed that a traditional roadmap couldn't have anticipated:

- Claude's capabilities improved significantly, making features possible that weren't possible when we started. We built them when they became possible, not according to a plan.
- User feedback revealed needs we hadn't identified at the outset. The features that emerged from user conversation are often the most valued.
- The market for AI products has shifted several times. What seemed like a differentiator six months ago is now table stakes; what didn't exist as a category then is now a meaningful market.

A roadmap built in March 2026 would have been wrong about what to build in July 2026. Not because we planned badly — because the landscape changed.

## What we use instead

We operate on a shorter planning horizon with a different structure:

**Principles** — the things we always care about, regardless of what we build. Our products are accurate above all else. Our UI is fast. Our AI output is verifiable, not opaque. These don't change.

**Current focus** — what we're working on right now, why, and what done looks like. Updated weekly. This is the equivalent of a sprint goal: specific, short-horizon, and informed by the most recent signal.

**Considered and ruled out** — a maintained list of things we've thought about building and decided not to. This is surprisingly valuable: it prevents us from relitigating decisions we've already made, and it's honest with users about what we're not going to do.

**Signal backlog** — user feedback, usage patterns, and market observations. Not a features list. We treat this as data, not commitments. We review it when making current focus decisions.

## The absence of a roadmap is not the absence of direction

This is the misunderstanding most often expressed about roadmap-free development: "how do you know where you're going?"

We know exactly where we're going at a principles level: we build accurate, fast AI products for professional users, with economics that allow a small team to run them sustainably. Every individual product decision sits against this direction.

What we don't know is the specific sequence of features over the next six months. We don't need to. The most important features reveal themselves through use, and the most important capabilities emerge from the technology.

Roadmaps answer the question "what are you building?" We answer that differently: "we're building whatever moves the product meaningfully toward its principles, informed by what users need and what's now possible."

## What roadmaps are actually for

Traditional roadmaps solve specific organisational problems:
- **Coordination**: multiple teams need to know what other teams are building to sequence their work
- **Investment**: investors and partners need to understand the future of the business
- **Customer commitment**: enterprise customers need to know what's coming to plan their own workflows
- **Stakeholder communication**: boards and advisors need context on direction

None of these problems apply to us yet. Two people don't need a roadmap for coordination — we talk every day. We're not raising investment. Our customers are mostly individual users who don't need feature commitments. Our stakeholders are ourselves.

The day we have organisational coordination problems, we'll need some version of a roadmap. The day we have enterprise customers who need feature commitments, we'll need to make some of those commitments. Until then, the roadmap is overhead without benefit.

## The risk

The risk of operating without a roadmap is drift: building things that are individually defensible but don't add up to a coherent product direction.

We mitigate this with the principles layer. Every feature has to connect to a principle. If we can't articulate why a feature advances the product's core principles, it doesn't go in. This is a harder bar than "it's on the roadmap."

The other risk: users might be uncertain about whether a feature they want is coming. We're transparent about this — we tell users honestly whether something is in our current focus, on our signal backlog, or something we've considered and ruled out. Honesty about uncertainty is better than a roadmap that implies false certainty.

## When we'll change this

We'll add more roadmap-like structure when:
- We have people who need to coordinate work
- We have customers who need feature commitments
- Our products are mature enough that the major strategic questions are settled

None of these are true yet. Until they are, the current approach is right for us.
