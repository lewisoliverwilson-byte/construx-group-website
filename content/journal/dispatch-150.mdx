Dispatch 150. We started this journal with an intention to write consistently and an expectation that we'd slow down after the first 20. We haven't slowed down.

Here's what we've learned from 150 dispatches about the practice of building in public.

## What the journal has actually done for the products

**Discovery.** The journal is the primary driver of organic discovery for all three products. Journal posts rank in search for technical queries ("Claude API structured output patterns", "eBay fee calculator TypeScript", "pgvector semantic search Next.js"). These readers are technically sophisticated and professionally aligned with the products.

**Credibility.** A developer or professional evaluating Scoutr, The Marqet, or The Hyve can read 150 posts about how we built them. The detail available about our architecture, decisions, and operational practices establishes a credibility that a marketing page can't.

**Recruitment.** Three people have reached out about potential roles after reading the journal. Two were the right fit. We haven't hired anyone yet (the team is still two), but having a pipeline of interested, pre-qualified people is an unexpected benefit.

**Clarity.** Writing about a decision forces you to articulate it. We've changed our minds about product decisions in the act of writing about them. The journal has been a thinking tool, not just a communication tool.

## What it has cost

**Time.** Each dispatch takes 45-90 minutes to write. 150 dispatches at an average of 60 minutes each is 150 hours — nearly four working weeks. This is the actual cost of the journal.

Is it worth it? We think so, based on the discovery and credibility benefits above. But it's a real cost that we're choosing to pay.

**Vulnerability.** Writing honestly about what's going wrong — the dispute we handled poorly, the onboarding failure, the technical debt we accumulated — makes us accountable in a way that curated marketing doesn't. This is intentional. It's also uncomfortable.

**Pace setting.** The discipline of publishing consistently creates a commitment. Missing a week feels like a failure even though no one is holding us to a schedule. The self-imposed pressure is real.

## What we'd tell ourselves starting over

**Start earlier.** We started the journal at launch. We should have started during development — the decisions made during the build phase are often the most interesting.

**Don't wait until something is finished to write about it.** The most useful posts we've written are about problems we're currently working through, not problems we've already solved. The in-process writing is more honest.

**Be specific.** The most-read and most-linked posts are the specific technical ones: the rate limiting implementation, the pgvector semantic search, the Stripe escrow pattern. Specific beats general every time.

**Don't self-censor on failures.** The posts about things that went wrong — the onboarding failures, the pricing iterations, the technical debt we regret — generate more reader response than the posts about successes. Readers are sophisticated; they know everything doesn't go right.

## The 150-dispatch marker

150 dispatches is an arbitrary number that doesn't mean much on its own. What it represents is a practice — writing consistently for months, about real work, with real detail.

The practice is more valuable than any individual post. The accumulation is more valuable than any single insight. The 150th dispatch benefits from the 149 before it: readers who arrive at a post find 149 more to read. The journal is a compound asset.

We'll keep writing. Not because we've planned how many more dispatches to write, but because the practice is generating value and we haven't found a reason to stop.

Dispatch 151 tomorrow.
