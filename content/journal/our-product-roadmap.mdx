A traditional product roadmap is a commitment: here's what we're building over the next six months, here's when it ships, here's what comes after. It requires predictability in capacity, stability in priorities, and confidence in user needs.

A two-person team running three live products has none of these. We've developed a roadmap process that fits what we actually are.

## What we're optimising for

Traditional roadmapping optimises for predictability: stakeholders know what's coming, teams can plan dependencies, commitments can be made and met. These are genuinely valuable properties — for organisations with multiple teams, external stakeholders, and long development cycles.

We're optimising for different things:
- **Learning rate**: we want to try things and find out what works quickly
- **Constraint honesty**: we want to know what we can't do, not just what we plan to do
- **Quality over volume**: fewer good changes shipped than many mediocre ones

The process we've built serves these properties.

## The three-tier system

We maintain three tiers of items, not one combined roadmap:

**Tier 1: Live incidents and bugs.** Things that are broken or degrading the experience for current users. These always take priority. No planning required — when something breaks, it gets fixed.

**Tier 2: The current sprint (2 weeks).** One to three items per product maximum. The constraint is real: we can't do more. Items get selected from Tier 3 based on user feedback, the decision log's outstanding questions, and what will create the most value.

**Tier 3: The backlog (unordered).** Every idea for every product. Not prioritised, not sequenced. This is where things go when we think they might be valuable but can't commit to when.

The key discipline: items don't graduate from Tier 3 to Tier 2 based on how long they've been waiting. They graduate based on the evidence that they're the right thing to build now. Something that's been in the backlog for four months doesn't have priority over something that entered last week and has strong user signal.

## How sprint items get selected

Every two weeks, we do a sprint review and planning session. It takes about 45 minutes. The agenda:

1. Review what shipped in the last sprint. Did it work? Did users engage with it? What changed?
2. Review any new user feedback, error reports, or usage data from the last two weeks.
3. Review the Tier 3 backlog for each product, looking for items where the evidence has strengthened.
4. Select the next sprint items. Be honest about capacity — two sprint weeks are usually 6-8 working days across both of us, shared across three products.
5. Log the selection rationale in the decision log.

The decision log step is important. "We chose to prioritise X over Y because Z" is a decision that matters. In six months, when someone asks why Y still hasn't shipped, the answer should be articulable.

## What goes into the backlog

The backlog capture rule: if the idea is worth remembering, write it down immediately. If it's not worth remembering, let it go.

We use Linear for The Hyve and The Marqet. Scoutr is managed in The Hyve itself (dog-fooding). Ideas get captured as backlog items with a brief description and the context that prompted them ("three users asked for this in the same week", "we noticed this when reviewing logs from Tuesday").

The context matters. A backlog item captured in six months with no context is often unactionable — you don't remember why it seemed important. An item with context lets you evaluate whether the original reasoning still holds.

## What we don't roadmap

We don't roadmap new ventures. The next venture emerges from pattern recognition across what we've built, not from a pre-planned development sequence. When the signal is strong enough and the capacity is there, a new venture starts.

We don't roadmap marketing. Blog posts, journal entries, and product launches happen when they happen. Adding them to a roadmap creates obligation without proportionate value.

We don't set quarterly OKRs. Objectives and key results work well for larger teams where shared direction needs explicit articulation. For two founders who talk every day, shared direction is a function of conversation, not documentation.

## The honest limitation

Our roadmap process doesn't give us multi-quarter visibility. We know what we're building this sprint. We have a sense of what might come next. We don't know what Q4 looks like, and we're honest about that.

The trade-off: we can't give users or partners a confident roadmap commitment. When someone asks "when will feature X ship?", the honest answer is often "it's in the backlog and we prioritise based on user feedback — we'll ship it when the evidence says it's the most valuable thing to build."

This honesty costs some credibility in conversations where commitments are expected. It gains trust in conversations where honesty is valued. We've found the right users tend to appreciate the latter.
