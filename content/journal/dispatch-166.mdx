Product-market fit is the concept that a product has found the right market — that demand exists, users find value, and growth is sustainable. It's also one of the most imprecisely used phrases in startup discourse.

Here's what we actually track, and what the data says about each product.

## The definitional problem

Marc Andreessen's original definition: "being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market." Sean Ellis popularised the "40% test": if more than 40% of users would be "very disappointed" if the product disappeared, you have product-market fit.

Both are useful framings with practical problems:

**"Good market with satisfying product"** tells you the outcome but not whether you have it.

**The 40% survey** requires users to respond to a survey in sufficient numbers, which requires having enough users to survey, which is the thing you don't have when you're most wondering whether you have PMF.

We track different things.

## Scoutr: the retention and cohort analysis

The clearest signal of product-market fit is retention. Users who find genuine value come back. Users who don't, churn.

For Scoutr, the signal we watch most closely:

**Week 4 retention:** Of users who activated in a given week (completed their first scan), what percentage are still scanning in week 4? For Scoutr, this is currently 61%. This is good. Most products see 20-30% at week 4.

**Usage frequency for retained users:** Users who stay with Scoutr past week 4 average 12 scans per active day. This is the signal of genuine workflow integration — the product is being used as part of a sourcing routine, not as a novelty.

**Qualitative: the "what if it disappeared?" question.** We ask this in offboarding surveys (when users cancel or go inactive). The most common response from cancellations isn't "I didn't find it valuable" — it's "I'm not actively sourcing right now." This is a temporary churn pattern, not a product rejection.

Scoutr's PMF signal is positive. The product is solving a real problem for a defined user segment with measurable retention and high usage frequency.

## The Marqet: the supply/demand balance

For a marketplace, PMF requires fit on both sides: buyers find the supply they need, sellers find the buyers they need. Imbalance in either direction produces a marketplace that technically functions but doesn't feel like PMF.

The signal we track:

**Time to first enquiry for new sellers:** Currently 8 days median for active listings. This is acceptable but not great. A well-functioning marketplace should see first enquiry within 2-3 days for a well-priced, well-described listing.

**Conversion rate (enquiry to transaction):** 34% of enquiries convert to a completed transaction. This is the demand-quality signal: buyers on The Marqet have genuine intent, not just browsing behaviour.

**Seller retention:** Sellers with at least one completed transaction in their first 60 days have 72% retention to month 6. Sellers without a transaction churn predictably. The product works for sellers who get results; it doesn't retain sellers who don't.

The PMF signal for The Marqet is that the product works when supply and demand are matched. The challenge is supply growth: the bottleneck is adding more quality sellers in the categories buyers want.

## The Hyve: the collaboration depth signal

For a team communication tool, PMF looks like genuine daily use and collaboration rather than exploratory use.

The signals we track:

**Daily Active Users / Monthly Active Users (DAU/MAU ratio):** At launch, this was 0.18 (users active ~5 days per month). Current: 0.54 (users active ~16 days per month). A ratio above 0.5 is often cited as strong engagement for a B2B tool.

**Message volume per active user per day:** 23 messages/day for active users. This indicates genuine conversation, not check-ins.

**AI agent usage:** 43% of active workspaces use the AI agent at least once per week. This is the differentiated feature; its usage suggests The Hyve is valued as an AI-first tool, not just a communication tool.

The Hyve's PMF signal is still developing. The engagement metrics are moving in the right direction, but the paying user base is small enough that cohort analysis isn't yet statistically reliable.

## What PMF feels like vs. what it looks like

The qualitative experience of approaching PMF: users tell you about problems you haven't solved yet because they're frustrated the product doesn't do more. This is the sign of a product someone is genuinely invested in.

The contrary: users who churn say nothing, or say "it's not for me." Users who have genuine PMF friction say "why doesn't it do X?" They're not leaving; they're asking for more.

We hear this most clearly for Scoutr: "Can it pull Amazon sales rank data?", "Will you add eBay category analysis?", "Can I save scan history to a spreadsheet?" These are the requests of users who use the product and want more of it.

PMF isn't a finish line. It's a state that your product can have more or less of, and that requires continuous work to maintain as the market evolves, as competitors respond, and as user expectations rise. The goal isn't to reach PMF and stop — it's to build enough of it that growth becomes easier.
