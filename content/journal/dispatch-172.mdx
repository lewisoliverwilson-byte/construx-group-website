Running three live products with two people is an exercise in context management. The products have different rhythms, different user problems, and different technical needs. Without a system, the result is reactive work: responding to wherever the most urgent fire is burning.

Here's the system we use.

## The weekly rhythm

Monday and Friday are the planning days. Monday: what are we working on this week across all three products? Friday: what shipped, what didn't, what moved to next week?

Tuesday through Thursday are uninterrupted work days. No cross-product context switching mid-day where avoidable. The structure:

- **Mornings:** The product that needs the most concentrated work that week. This rotates based on what's in flight.
- **Afternoons:** The other products, in shorter blocks. Responding to user feedback, deploying small fixes, reviewing metrics.

The discipline of not switching context mid-morning is the most important part. A morning of concentrated work on a single codebase is more productive than three hours split across three codebases.

## The "red board" rule

Each week, each product gets a "red board" — the one thing it needs that week. Not the five things it needs; the one thing.

Scoutr's red board might be: "Improve the confidence score explanation for edge cases." The Marqet's might be: "Fix the mobile layout on listing search results." The Hyve's might be: "Ship the channel archive feature."

The red board prevents the "good enough progress on many things" trap — the week where you made partial progress on seven things and finished none of them. Three red boards, three weeks of work that shipped.

## The async-first communication pattern

Two founders in the same product universe have a tendency to solve problems in conversation rather than in code. The conversation feels like work; the code is the work.

We've moved product discussions to async by default. When we have a product question — "should the Hyve archive feature notify members?" — we write it down, consider it separately, and resolve it with a short written answer rather than a synchronous discussion.

The written resolution becomes the decision log. A month later, when we wonder why we made a particular choice, the written answer is there.

## Product ownership as context management

Each founder has primary ownership of specific products:

- Scoutr: primarily my context
- The Marqet and The Hyve: shared context

Primary ownership doesn't mean exclusive ownership — both of us can and do work on all three products. It means one person is the default decision-maker and context-holder for that product. Questions about Scoutr come to me first; I don't have to context-switch to answer them.

This reduces the "two people understand everything at half-depth" problem that plagues small teams.

## What breaks the system

**User-facing incidents.** A production incident is a legitimate context switch. When something breaks, you drop the planned work and fix it. The system is designed for normal operating conditions; incidents bypass it.

**External commitments.** A call with a potential partner, a customer conversation, a tax filing — these interrupt the week's rhythm. We schedule external commitments on Monday or Friday where possible.

**The "one more thing" failure mode.** "While I'm in this codebase, let me also fix this other thing." This is where planned morning work expands to fill the day. The fix: write down the other thing, don't do it now.

## The load measurement

Two products in active development + one in maintenance mode is manageable. Three products in active development is at the edge of what two people can sustain without quality suffering.

Currently: Scoutr is in maintenance-plus-feature mode (core product stable, adding features based on user requests). The Marqet is in growth mode (the core works, the focus is user acquisition and seller onboarding quality). The Hyve is in active development (core works, building differentiated features).

If all three were in active development simultaneously, something would suffer. The system works because the products are in different phases.

## The meta-lesson

Systems for managing multiple products don't prevent the work from being hard. They prevent the work from being chaotic. The red board, the weekly rhythm, and the async communication pattern don't reduce the total amount of work — they make sure the work that gets done is the right work.

The alternative to systems is intuition about priorities, which degrades under load. When everything is urgent, you need a structure to decide what's actually most important this week. The system is that structure.
