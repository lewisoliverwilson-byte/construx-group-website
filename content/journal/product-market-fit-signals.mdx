Product-market fit is one of the most overloaded terms in startup thinking. It's described as a threshold you cross, a moment you know, a state the company enters. In practice it's a spectrum, a set of signals, and almost always more product-specific than market-specific.

After six months with three products at different stages, here's what we've learned about reading fit signals.

## The most reliable early signal: pull vs. push

The weakest evidence of fit: customers who need convincing to stay.

The strongest evidence: customers who are annoyed when the product is unavailable, who tell others without being asked, who contact you requesting features rather than waiting to be asked for feedback.

We call this pull vs. push. Push is when you're pushing the product to maintain retention. Pull is when the product's value is pulling users back on its own. Pull is a fit signal. Push means you're compensating for a fit gap.

Scoutr started as mostly push — we were sending usage reminders, following up with inactive users, manually checking in with trial users. Over three months, we added product improvements based on the feedback from those conversations. The ratio shifted. Users started reaching out without prompts. That shift was the first clear fit signal.

## Fit is product-specific, not company-specific

A company can have strong product-market fit for one product and weak fit for another. We're experiencing this simultaneously.

Scoutr's fit signal is stronger than The Hyve's at this stage. Scoutr has a clearer target user (Amazon resellers with consistent margins questions), a specific repeated workflow (scan before buying), and measurable outcomes (did the product help them find profitable products). The feedback loop is tight and the use case is specific.

The Hyve's fit signal is weaker. The product serves a broader category (teams using AI collaboratively), the use case is less defined (AI-assisted project work), and the outcomes are less measurable (how do you quantify whether the AI agent improved team decisions?). This doesn't mean The Hyve won't find fit — it means the fit signal requires more work to interpret and more time to emerge.

Reading fit requires understanding which product is at which stage, not whether the company as a whole has fit.

## Cohort retention as a fit proxy

The metric that matters most for early-stage fit: what percentage of users who started in month 1 are still active in month 3?

Churn tells you about the past. Cohort retention predicts the future. A product with high early churn but improving cohort retention over time is trending toward fit. A product with stable but low retention is stuck.

We track 30-day, 60-day, and 90-day retention by cohort for each product. The Marqet's 30-day retention is the strongest of the three — users who find a listing they want keep coming back to check for new ones. This is a meaningful fit signal for the content directory model.

## The Sean Ellis question, adapted for B2B

Sean Ellis's original product-market fit test: survey users with "how would you feel if you could no longer use this product?" and target >40% "very disappointed."

This is a useful benchmark but requires adaptation for B2B tools with specific professional workflows.

For Scoutr, the more useful version: "how would it affect your product sourcing process if Scoutr were unavailable tomorrow?" The framing shifts from emotional response (disappointed) to practical impact (workflow effect). For professional tools, workflow impact is a better fit proxy than emotional response.

We've run this informally in customer conversations. The responses that indicate fit: "I'd go back to spreadsheets and manual fee calculation, which would take significantly longer." Responses that indicate fit gaps: "I'd probably use a different tool" or "it would slow things down a bit."

## The problem with misattributed fit

A common mistake: attributing fit signals to the wrong cause.

High retention because the product is genuinely excellent and users find it valuable → fit signal.

High retention because users are locked in by imported data or complex setup → switching cost, not fit signal.

High conversion from trial to paid because the pricing is low → pricing success, not fit signal.

We've been careful to distinguish these. Scoutr's retention improvement coincided with shipping a batch scanning feature that professional users wanted — there's a causal link between the product improvement and the retention change. If retention had improved after we reduced prices, we'd be less confident it indicated fit.

## Negative fit signals that look positive

The most dangerous fit indicator: users who say they love the product but don't pay, don't return, or don't refer.

Enthusiasm during demos, positive feedback in conversations, social media mentions — none of these are fit signals unless they translate to the behaviours that indicate actual value: repeat use, payment, and referral.

We've had users who were enthusiastic early adopters who contributed almost nothing to retention metrics. Their enthusiasm was real; their need for the product was not deep enough to create sustained use.

The behaviours matter more than the words. Fit is revealed in what users do, not what they say.

## Where we are

Scoutr: moderate early fit signals, improving retention, specific user segment with clear use case. The fit story is becoming clearer.

The Marqet: strong discovery and first-use metrics, more ambiguous retention. The content directory model works at acquisition; the question is whether users return as frequently as the model needs.

The Hyve: early stage, weaker fit signals, broader use case. The product is newer and the signal requires more time to read.

None of these are failed products — they're at different stages of fit development. Understanding where each one is allows us to allocate development effort where the fit gap is most critical.
