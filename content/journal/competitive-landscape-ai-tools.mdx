Competitive analysis is a ritual in most product companies. Quarterly competitive reviews, feature comparison matrices, positioning maps. Most of it produces documents that inform nothing and sit in shared drives unread.

Here's our more minimal take on competition.

## Scoutr: the competitive position

Scoutr operates in the Amazon FBA research tools market. The established players are Helium 10, Jungle Scout, Viral Launch, and a dozen smaller tools. These are well-funded, mature products with deep feature sets and large user bases.

Scoutr doesn't compete with them on breadth. Those tools do keyword research, listing optimisation, inventory management, and supplier sourcing — a complete Amazon seller platform. Scoutr does one thing: scans product pages and calculates whether products are worth buying for resale.

The competitive positioning: Scoutr is for resellers (retail arbitrage, online arbitrage), not for private label sellers. Helium 10 and Jungle Scout are optimised for private label — finding products to manufacture and sell under your own brand. Resellers have different needs: fast scan of many products, real-time fee calculation, multi-platform comparison (Amazon + eBay).

This is a genuine market gap. The established tools don't serve resellers well because resellers aren't their core user. We're not displacing Helium 10; we're serving a segment it largely ignores.

## The Marqet: first-mover advantage in a new category

The Marqet's competitive landscape is thin because the category barely exists. There are a few other marketplaces for AI prompts, but none focused specifically on professional Claude configurations with the depth of listing quality we've built.

The risk: the category doesn't exist yet, which means the demand isn't proven. The advantage: no established competitor with data moats or user loyalty to overcome.

First-mover advantage in a new category is real but temporary. If AI configuration marketplaces become a significant market, larger players (Anthropic's own marketplace, Amazon's AI product ecosystem) will enter. Our moat until that point: catalogue quality, creator relationships, and the trust we build with buyers.

## The Hyve: the most competitive market

The Hyve is in the team collaboration + AI tools market, and this is genuinely competitive. Notion, Linear, Slack, GitHub Discussions, Discord — large established products with deep moat. And Notion AI, Slack AI, GitHub Copilot — the incumbents are all adding AI.

Our position: we're not competing with Notion and Slack for enterprise customers. We're competing for small, AI-first builder teams (under 15 people) who are early enough in their tool adoption that they haven't committed to the incumbent stack, and who care enough about AI-native collaboration to switch for it.

This is a specific segment. Whether it's large enough to build a business on is still being tested.

## The framework we actually use

Instead of competitive analysis in the traditional sense, we ask three questions:

**Who has this problem right now?** This is market sizing filtered through specificity. "AI tools users" is too broad. "Amazon resellers who source products manually and want to be faster" is specific enough to be useful.

**What are they currently using?** This reveals the real competition — not other tools in our category, but whatever the user is doing instead. Scoutr's real competition is a spreadsheet and manual fee calculation. The Marqet's real competition is spending 3 hours on prompt engineering. The Hyve's real competition is switching between 4 tabs constantly.

**Why would they switch?** This is the only question that matters for positioning. The answer has to be specific and compelling, not generic ("better AI"). Scoutr's answer: "5 seconds instead of 20 minutes, with more accuracy." Specific and verifiable.

## Why we don't do competitive analysis

Quarterly competitive reviews don't change our decisions. We know who our competitors are; we monitor them occasionally. What changes our decisions is user feedback, retention data, and our own product intuition.

The time spent on competitive analysis is time not spent on the product. At our scale and with three products to develop, the marginal value of competitive analysis is very low compared to the marginal value of shipping something that users want.

We check on competitors when: a user mentions a competitor in a support message, a competitor ships something significant that affects our positioning, or we're considering a major product direction change that would move us into a more competitive space.

Outside of those triggers, we focus inward.
