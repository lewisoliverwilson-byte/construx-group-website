We have no customer support function. All user contact comes to a shared inbox that both founders see. We respond to everything.

This is sustainable at our current user count. It won't scale indefinitely. Here's what the reality of no-team support looks like and what we've learned from it.

## The actual volume

Across three products, we receive approximately 30-40 user messages per week. These include:

- Bug reports (about 20%)
- Feature requests (about 25%)
- "How does this work?" questions (about 30%)
- General feedback and responses to our journal posts (about 15%)
- Churn messages ("cancelling because...") (about 10%)

30-40 messages per week is manageable for two founders who both have email open. We aim to respond within 4 hours during working hours, 24 hours otherwise.

## What the support inbox is really for

The support inbox is product research.

Every "how does this work?" message is a gap in onboarding or documentation. Every bug report is a technical issue that probably affects more users than the one who reported it. Every churn message is a cancellation reason that informs retention work. Every feature request is evidence of unmet need.

We tag every message by type in Linear and review the patterns monthly. When "how do I export scan results?" appears five times in a month, we improve the export documentation. When "the scan gave me wrong results for eBay" appears repeatedly, it becomes a P2 bug.

The inbox is a low-cost, high-signal feedback channel that most teams discard by routing all support through an automated FAQ system. The FAQ might reduce volume, but it removes the signal.

## The tools we use

**Email**: the primary support channel. We handle support in a shared email inbox. Nothing fancy — just email, forwarding, and tags.

**Linear**: where bug reports and feature requests land. When a user reports a bug, we create a Linear issue immediately. If the same bug appears twice, we link both user messages to the same issue. The frequency of an issue's appearance weights its priority.

**Loom**: for complex issues where text explanation is slow, we record a short Loom screen recording showing how to accomplish something. This is faster for us to create than a detailed written explanation and easier for the user to follow. We send the Loom link in the reply.

**The journal**: the posts most relevant to user questions eventually become linked in support replies. "Here's a post that explains how we think about this" is a more complete answer than a one-off email explanation.

## The churn message pattern

About 10% of user contact is churn-related — "I'm cancelling because..." messages. These are the most important messages we receive.

The pattern we've noticed over six months:

**Scoutr churn reasons**: "found the products but the margins weren't there" (product and market issue, not a Scoutr issue), "too expensive for occasional use" (pricing/value positioning issue), "eBay results weren't accurate" (product quality issue, fixable).

**The Marqet churn reasons**: "bought a listing and it wasn't what I expected" (listing quality or description accuracy issue), "not using Claude Projects enough to justify the purchase" (distribution issue — users who don't use Claude Projects regularly aren't the right buyer).

**The Hyve churn**: early product, churn reasons are less consolidated, but the most common is "team didn't adopt it" (team change management issue, hard to address from our side).

We've made product changes based on churn analysis: improved eBay calculation accuracy in Scoutr, added preview content to Marqet listings to reduce description-expectation gap, improved The Hyve's team invitation and onboarding flow.

## The response quality bar

Every support response is an opportunity to maintain or repair a user relationship. We don't send template replies.

Our response structure for most issues:
1. Acknowledge the specific issue (demonstrate we read the message)
2. Explain what's happening (if it's a known issue or a usage question)
3. Tell them what we're doing about it (if it's a bug, when they can expect a fix)
4. Offer anything we can do to help immediately (if applicable)
5. Thank them for the report

For bug reports, we close the loop: when the fix ships, we email the user who reported it. "You reported a bug with eBay pricing calculations last month — we've fixed it in today's update." This is simple and it produces remarkably positive responses. Users are surprised when companies follow up on bug reports.

## Where this breaks

The current approach breaks at scale. Handling 150-200 user messages per week while also building products would be unsustainable. We're currently at a scale where it works; we have no clear plan for when it stops working.

The options at that scale: hire a part-time customer support person, invest heavily in documentation and self-service so message volume doesn't grow proportionally with users, or build some level of AI-assisted response routing. All three are on the medium-term list; none are immediate priorities.

For now: the inbox stays open, we read everything, and we respond personally. The signal quality is worth the time.
