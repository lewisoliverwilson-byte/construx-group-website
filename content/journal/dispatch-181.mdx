Disputes are the most sensitive user experience in any marketplace. A buyer who raises a dispute is unhappy and at risk of churn. A seller facing an unfair dispute is at risk of leaving. The resolution system has to be fair, fast, and trust-preserving for both parties.

Here's how we designed it.

## The trigger conditions

A dispute can be raised by a buyer when:

1. The work was completed but doesn't match the agreed brief
2. The seller became unresponsive after payment was captured
3. The seller is unable to complete the work due to circumstances outside the buyer's control

Disputes cannot be raised over price disagreements established before the engagement began, stylistic preferences not covered in the brief, or late completions where no deadline was agreed.

We're explicit about these conditions in the dispute initiation flow. Clear conditions reduce frivolous disputes and set appropriate expectations for buyers who have a legitimate grievance.

## The escrow design

The dispute system is built on the escrow architecture described in an earlier dispatch. In brief:

1. Buyer initiates payment — funds captured from card
2. Funds held in a Stripe PaymentIntent with `capture_method: 'manual'`
3. On work completion, buyer confirms — Stripe PaymentIntent is captured and transferred to seller
4. If buyer raises a dispute before confirmation — escrow holds while the dispute resolves

This means a dispute never involves money that's already been paid out. The dispute resolution decides where the held funds go — to the seller (if the buyer's claim is unfounded) or back to the buyer (if the seller failed to deliver).

## The dispute flow

**Step 1: Buyer raises dispute.** Submits a description, selects the dispute reason, and optionally attaches evidence (screenshots, the agreed brief document).

**Step 2: Seller notified.** The seller has 48 hours to respond. They can either:
- Accept the dispute (refund to buyer)
- Contest the dispute (submit their perspective and evidence)

**Step 3: 72-hour negotiation window.** Both parties can communicate through the dispute thread. We encourage self-resolution — most disputes resolve here when both parties communicate directly.

**Step 4: Platform review (if not self-resolved).** We review the evidence and make a decision within 5 business days. Decisions are final.

```ts
type DisputeStatus =
  | 'raised'              // Buyer has raised, awaiting seller response
  | 'seller_responding'   // Within seller's 48h response window
  | 'in_negotiation'      // Both parties in the 72h window
  | 'under_review'        // Platform reviewing
  | 'resolved_buyer'      // Resolved in buyer's favour (refund)
  | 'resolved_seller'     // Resolved in seller's favour (payment released)
  | 'resolved_split';     // Partial refund (split outcome)
```

The split outcome is used when both parties share responsibility — for example, the buyer provided an unclear brief and the seller delivered accordingly. In this case, we may decide on a 50% refund.

## The decision framework

When a dispute reaches platform review, we evaluate:

**Was the brief clear?** A vague brief protects neither party. If the delivered work technically matches the brief (even if the buyer is dissatisfied), the dispute is harder for the buyer to win.

**Was there communication about problems?** A seller who encountered problems and communicated them proactively is in a better position than one who went silent.

**Is there documented evidence of the agreed outcome?** Disputes with a written brief and scope are much easier to resolve than disputes based on verbal agreements or assumptions.

**Is there a pattern?** A seller with multiple disputes is a signal. A buyer who has raised multiple disputes is also a signal. We maintain dispute history in our risk system.

## The notification system

Both parties receive notifications at each stage:

- Dispute raised: immediate notification to seller
- Seller response received: immediate notification to buyer
- 48h with no seller response: escalate automatically to platform review
- 24h before negotiation window closes: reminder to both parties
- Platform decision: immediate notification to both parties with reasoning

The reasoning letter is important: we explain why the decision was made, referencing the specific evidence considered. Unexplained decisions feel arbitrary; explained decisions feel fair even when unfavourable.

## The data layer

```sql
CREATE TABLE disputes (
  id          uuid PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
  order_id    uuid REFERENCES orders(id) NOT NULL,
  raised_by   uuid REFERENCES users(id) NOT NULL,
  reason      text NOT NULL,
  status      dispute_status NOT NULL DEFAULT 'raised',
  
  buyer_statement    text,
  seller_statement   text,
  platform_decision  text,
  decision_reason    text,
  
  resolution         text, -- 'buyer' | 'seller' | 'split'
  split_pct          numeric(5,2), -- If split: buyer refund %
  
  raised_at          timestamptz DEFAULT now(),
  seller_responded_at timestamptz,
  resolved_at         timestamptz,
  deadline            timestamptz GENERATED ALWAYS AS (raised_at + INTERVAL '7 days') STORED
);
```

The `deadline` column is generated — 7 days from raise. Our monitoring checks for disputes approaching their deadline and escalates if not yet resolved.

## Results

Dispute rate: 2.1% of completed transactions.

Resolution breakdown:
- Self-resolved (buyer/seller directly): 58%
- Resolved in buyer's favour: 26%
- Resolved in seller's favour: 11%
- Split resolution: 5%

The 58% self-resolution rate is the most important metric. It means the system is working as designed: providing a structured space for parties to resolve disagreements themselves, with platform review as a backstop.

The 2.1% dispute rate is within industry norms for a new marketplace. We expect this to improve as we refine the brief-writing guidance (which prevents many disputes) and the seller vetting process (which reduces the incidence of non-delivery).
