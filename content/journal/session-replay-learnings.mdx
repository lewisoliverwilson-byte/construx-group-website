Metrics tell you what happened. Session replay shows you why.

We added PostHog session replay to all three products about three months into running them. The decision to record and watch user sessions felt slightly uncomfortable at first — users should know, which is why we document it in our privacy policy. But the insight is significant enough that it's now one of our standard product development inputs.

Here's what we actually learned, not the generic "we found UX issues" version.

## The form abandonment pattern

Scoutr's onboarding has a multi-step form: name, email, plan selection, payment. Metrics showed a drop-off between plan selection and payment at a rate that suggested users were clicking to the payment step and leaving.

Session replay showed something different. Users were clicking "continue to payment," the page was navigating to the Stripe checkout, and a significant fraction were immediately pressing browser back. Not because they decided not to pay — because Stripe's checkout opened, they saw a field they'd have to fill in, and they wanted to check something in their email or bank app first.

The metric showed "payment abandonment." The session showed "interrupted intent." Completely different problems with completely different solutions.

Our fix: we added a "you'll complete payment on a secure Stripe page — you'll need your card details" note on the plan selection step, before the user clicks through. Drop-off at payment improved by 18% within two weeks. The intervention cost 30 minutes to implement.

## The feature discovery gap

The Marqet's listing management interface has a bulk edit feature accessible via a small button in the top-right corner of the listings table. Metrics showed the feature was barely used.

Session replay showed users looking at individual listings, editing them one by one, pausing, scrolling up, not finding anything useful, and going back to individual edits. Not once in the sessions we watched did a user find the bulk edit button.

We moved the bulk edit control to a sticky bar that appears when the user selects multiple rows. Within two weeks, bulk edit usage increased by 8x. The feature hadn't changed — the discoverability had.

## The mobile scroll trap

The Hyve's workspace has a document panel that scrolls independently of the main viewport. On desktop, this is obvious from the visual design. On mobile, it wasn't obvious at all.

Session replay of mobile sessions showed a consistent pattern: users would try to scroll the page, find themselves scrolling the document panel, become confused about what was happening, and either stop using the feature or close the app.

We hadn't tested this interaction carefully on mobile because we'd assumed (incorrectly) that the visual design was clear enough. Session replay made the failure concrete: not a theory about what might be confusing, but video evidence of users being confused.

The fix: visual cues (a subtle scrollbar always visible on mobile, and a gradient fade at the bottom of the panel) and a brief tooltip on first use. Mobile engagement with documents increased meaningfully.

## The copy-paste behaviour

Scoutr's scan results include a margin calculation breakdown. We'd assumed users would read this in-app and make decisions there.

Session replay showed something we hadn't anticipated: users were selecting the text from the scan result and copying it into a spreadsheet. Not using our UI to export or share — manually selecting and copying text.

This told us two things. First, users were integrating Scoutr's data into a broader workflow that we didn't support. Second, the data format we presented for in-app reading wasn't the format they wanted for external use.

We added a "copy to clipboard" button with a structured CSV format of the results. We're also building a proper data export feature. Neither of these would have been obvious from click metrics alone — the insight came from watching users do something we hadn't designed for.

## The rage click clusters

PostHog shows "rage click" patterns — where users click multiple times on something they expect to be interactive but isn't.

We found rage clicks on:
- The Hyve's loading state (users clicking an element that was in a loading state, expecting it to respond)
- Scoutr's scan status indicator (users clicking an in-progress indicator, expecting to see more detail)
- The Marqet's category labels (users clicking labels that looked like links but weren't)

Each rage click cluster indicated an unmet expectation. Either the element looked clickable and wasn't, or it appeared to be doing something and users wanted more information about what.

We fixed each one: the loading state became a skeleton with a progress indicator, the scan status indicator became a tooltip with detail on hover, and the category labels in The Marqet became clickable filters.

## What we don't watch

We don't watch sessions from users who have opted out of analytics. We don't watch sessions that contain sensitive data (payment screens, personal information inputs). PostHog makes this configurable — we mask specific elements.

The scope of what we watch is: navigation behaviour, feature discovery, interaction with UI elements, and general task flow. We're looking for UX failures, not personal information.

## The practice

Session replay is now part of our fortnightly product review. We set aside 30-45 minutes every two weeks to watch 10-15 sessions across the three products. We look specifically for:
- Rage clicks and repeated interactions that suggest confusion
- Abandonment points — where users stop and leave
- Unexpected workflows — things users are doing that we didn't anticipate
- Mobile vs. desktop behaviour differences

The sessions we learn the most from are the ones where users do something we didn't design for. These are the opportunities — either a workflow we should support, or a failure mode we should fix.

No amount of analytics tells you what session replay shows you in 2 minutes of watching a real user struggle with something that should be obvious.
