The classic marketplace cold start problem: value comes from liquidity (many buyers and sellers transacting), but you have no liquidity on day one. Either side of the market is useless without the other. You have to pick one to start.

For The Marqet — a services marketplace for professional freelancers — we chose supply first. Here's the reasoning and the execution.

## Why supply first

For most services marketplaces, supply (sellers) is harder to recruit than demand (buyers). Buyers follow supply — people go where the professionals they want are. Professionals are selective about where they invest time in a profile and portfolio.

If we'd built buyer demand first, we'd have buyers waiting on a marketplace with no sellers. Buyers churn immediately when they don't find what they want. Seller recruitment could follow, but by then we'd have burned our first-impression window.

The reverse: recruit sellers into a curated initial cohort, build their profiles carefully, then bring buyers to a marketplace with credible supply. Buyers arriving to find quality sellers have a reason to complete a transaction. That first transaction produces the feedback loop (seller earns, buyer gets work done, both return) that sustains marketplace growth.

## Recruiting the first sellers

We didn't launch with open sign-ups. We recruited 15 sellers directly — people we knew or could reach through professional networks who had services we believed buyers would want.

The pitch for early sellers: we'll help you build your profile, you'll be featured prominently on a new platform, your early clients will pay full price and receive full service, and you'll have early mover advantage in your category before the platform scales.

What we did for each seller:

- Helped write the listing copy (first drafts, edited, reviewed against what we'd seen convert on comparable platforms)
- Took professional headshots for sellers who didn't have them (this made a material difference to profile quality)
- Verified their credentials before launch (we required verification from day one — credibility of the initial cohort mattered)
- Set realistic pricing expectations (neither too low as a fake introductory offer, nor too high for an unproven platform)

The 15-seller initial cohort took 6 weeks to recruit and onboard.

## Bringing the first buyers

With 15 quality sellers live, we opened to buyers via a waitlist-first model. The waitlist served two purposes:

**Demand staging:** We could open the platform to cohorts of buyers rather than everyone at once. This let us observe and fix early friction before scale.

**Perceived exclusivity:** A waitlist signals that the platform is worth waiting for. This is a legitimate signal when the supply is genuinely curated — you're telling buyers "we've built something good."

The initial buyer acquisition was direct outreach. We contacted 200 people who we knew had bought or needed professional services in the categories our initial sellers covered (design, copywriting, digital marketing, development). We pitched the platform personally.

From 200 outreach messages: 78 waitlist sign-ups, 24 approved for the initial cohort, 9 completed transactions in the first month.

Nine transactions is not traction. But 9 real transactions through a new marketplace in month 1 is enough to learn, enough to show sellers the platform is active, and enough to iterate from.

## The category focus decision

We didn't launch all possible service categories. We launched with five:
- Brand design
- Copywriting
- Performance marketing
- Web development
- Business strategy consulting

These weren't chosen arbitrarily. They were chosen because:
1. We had strong candidates for initial sellers in these categories
2. These categories have good product-market fit for digital delivery (no logistics, no in-person requirements)
3. The buyer demographics we could reach (startup founders, small business owners) buy these services

Category focus made the supply-side recruitment tractable. Finding 3 great brand designers is feasible. Finding 3 great brand designers, 3 great plumbers, 3 great caterers, and 3 great wedding photographers simultaneously is not.

## The single-category depth play

Within the 5 categories, we focused hardest on brand design. Three reasons:

1. **High AOV:** Brand design engagements typically run £500-3,000. Fewer transactions needed to demonstrate platform health.
2. **Portfolio-visible:** Design work is visual and shareable. Good outcomes are easy to show prospective sellers and buyers.
3. **Network density:** Designers know other designers. A great experience for one designer increases the chance of a referral to another.

The first 3 of our 9 month-1 transactions were brand design. The sellers from those transactions referred two additional sellers.

## Month 6 state

Six months after launch:
- 47 active sellers across the initial 5 categories
- 4 new categories opened (SEO, video production, financial consulting, PR)
- 180+ completed transactions
- Seller repeat rate: 73% (sellers who accept a project return for a second)
- Buyer repeat rate: 41%

The 41% buyer repeat rate is the number we're most focused on improving. Single-transaction buyers leave money on the table — the platform earns only one fee, the seller earns only one engagement, and we don't compound.

## What the cold start actually looks like

Theory of cold start says: pick a side, solve liquidity for that side, then attract the other side. Practice of cold start looks like: 6 weeks of hard recruitment, 200 outreach messages, 9 transactions, and then slowly making the flywheel turn.

The flywheel is real, but it takes more pushes to get started than the theory suggests. The important thing is that the first push has to produce something good — not just presence on the platform, but a completed transaction that both sides would recommend. One genuine success story is worth more than 50 sign-ups.
