Most teams make the same decision three times.

Once when they first face it — someone brings it up in a meeting or a thread, everyone weighs in, a conclusion is reached. Once when they forget they made it — a new thread opens, the same arguments resurface, the same conclusion is reached again an hour later. And once when a new person joins and asks why things are the way they are — and nobody can find where the original reasoning lives.

This is a solved problem for teams that are rigorous about documentation. It is an unsolved problem for everyone else, which is most teams.

The Hyve's decision engine is a product attempt to fix this without requiring the team to be disciplined about documentation.

## What the decision engine does

When a question comes up in a Hyve channel — "should we charge upfront or on delivery?", "which API should we use for X?", "what's our policy on Y?" — the AI team member does two things.

First, it checks the decision log. If this question has been asked and answered before, it surfaces the previous decision before anyone has a chance to re-open the debate. "We discussed this on 14 March. The conclusion was [X] because [Y]. Does this still hold?"

Second, if it's a new question and the team reaches a conclusion, it asks whether to log it. One click writes the decision, the reasoning, and the context into the project's decision archive with a timestamp and attribution.

Neither step requires a separate workflow. The decision engine lives in the same thread where work happens. The friction of logging is near zero; the value of retrieval is high.

## The retrieval problem

The hard part of building this wasn't the logging. It was making retrieval good enough to be useful.

The naive approach — search for keyword overlap between new questions and archived decisions — fails badly in practice. Teams don't phrase questions consistently. "What payment processor do we use?" and "have we decided on Stripe yet?" are asking about the same thing. A keyword search that treats them as different queries is not useful.

The Hyve uses vector search over the decision archive. Each decision is embedded at write time. When a new question comes in, the AI embeds it and finds semantically similar decisions — decisions that are *about* the same thing, regardless of how they were phrased.

This dramatically improves recall. The system doesn't surface every past decision every time — only ones that are genuinely relevant to the current question, ranked by semantic similarity.

The threshold matters. Too sensitive and the engine interrupts constantly with tangentially related past decisions. Too loose and it misses things. The current calibration surfaces decisions when cosine similarity is above a threshold we tuned against a set of test cases — questions we knew were and weren't related to archived decisions.

## What gets logged

Not every conversation produces a decision worth archiving. "What time is the call?" doesn't need to go in the decision log. "We're switching to fortnightly cadence starting next week" does.

Distinguishing these is a classification problem. The AI is looking for conclusions that have forward-looking implications — things that constrain future choices, establish patterns, or set precedents. Scheduling arrangements, individual tasks, and quick-turnaround factual questions generally don't qualify.

The AI is also watching for language signals: "we've decided," "going forward," "the policy will be," "we're going with," "let's lock in." These aren't perfect signals — teams speak informally — but they're good priors for when to ask about logging.

## Context that makes decisions useful

A decision logged without context is a fact without meaning. "We use Stripe" is less useful than "We use Stripe because the API is better documented than Braintree and we already have it integrated from the Scoutr project — switching cost would be high."

The Hyve records four fields per decision:

**The question** — what was actually being decided. Often rephrased by the AI into a clean question form from the messy conversation.

**The conclusion** — the outcome. What was decided.

**The reasoning** — why. This is the most valuable field for future retrieval, because it tells you whether the conclusion still holds. "We chose X because Y" — if Y is no longer true, the decision is probably worth revisiting.

**The context** — who was involved, when, and what prompted the question. Useful for knowing how authoritative the decision is: a quick async agreement is different from a deliberate team discussion.

## How a new team member uses it

The highest-value use case for the decision engine isn't in-team retrieval. It's onboarding.

A new person joining a project needs to understand not just what the decisions are, but why things are the way they are. That reasoning lives in the decision archive in a structured, searchable form.

Instead of spending their first two weeks asking "why do we do it this way?" and getting inconsistent answers from whoever happens to be around, they can ask the AI team member directly. "What decisions have been made about the pricing model?" returns the archive entries with full reasoning, in order of relevance.

This is documentation that writes itself — not because anyone disciplined decided to maintain it, but because the cost of maintaining it is low enough that it happens as a side effect of normal work.

## The meta-problem it solves

Teams waste enormous time on context reconstruction — figuring out why something is the way it is before they can make an intelligent change to it.

Code has git blame. Databases have migration histories. Product decisions have nothing — or they have scattered Notion pages, dead Slack threads, and the memory of whoever was in the meeting.

The decision engine doesn't require a separate knowledge management workflow. It sits in the tool where work happens and captures the reasoning before it evaporates. That's the design constraint we optimised for: zero friction on write, high value on read.

The test of whether it's working is simple: are teams asking "didn't we decide this already?" less often? In the teams using The Hyve, they are.
