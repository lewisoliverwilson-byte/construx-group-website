Metrics are a choice. You can measure everything (PostHog makes this possible) or you can choose a small set of numbers that genuinely indicate product health. We've gravitated toward the latter — three or four numbers per product that tell us whether we're winning or losing.

Here's what we track and why.

## The metrics hierarchy

There's a useful distinction between:

**Vanity metrics:** Numbers that look impressive but don't correlate with business health. Page views, social media followers, registered user count (without usage).

**Activity metrics:** Numbers that indicate what's happening. Events, session counts, feature usage rates. Useful for debugging and optimisation; not sufficient for understanding business health.

**Health metrics:** Numbers that directly indicate whether the product is working. These are product-specific and hard to fake — they reflect whether users are getting real value.

We track all three types but optimise for health metrics.

## Scoutr's health metrics

**Scans per active user per week.** This is the primary engagement metric. A user who scans 20 products per week is getting regular value from Scoutr. A user who scanned twice and stopped isn't. Target: 10+ scans/week for active users.

**Confidence score distribution.** If all scans return low confidence, users will stop trusting the tool. We track the distribution of confidence scores (0-29 / 30-59 / 60-79 / 80+). Healthy distribution has meaningful representation across all zones.

**Scan→action rate.** What fraction of high-confidence scans result in a purchase (tracked via a user-reported purchase confirmation). This validates whether the confidence score is predictive of real purchase decisions.

**Retention at 30 days.** What fraction of users who scanned in month 1 are still scanning in month 2. Target: 40%+.

What we **don't** track:
- Total scans (an absolute number that grows as we add users; not informative about per-user engagement)
- Time-on-page (Scoutr is a tool; fast is good, not a sign of disengagement)
- NPS in the abstract (we ask users for feedback, but through outreach, not automated surveys)

## The Marqet's health metrics

**Transaction completion rate.** Of inquiries that become contracts, what fraction complete without dispute or abandonment? Target: 90%+. Below 85% indicates friction in the escrow/completion flow or trust problems.

**Buyer repeat rate at 90 days.** What fraction of buyers who complete a first transaction complete a second within 90 days? Target: 40%+. Repeat buyers indicate genuine value delivered.

**Seller retention at 60 days.** What fraction of sellers who list and receive their first contract are still active 60 days later? Target: 65%+. Seller churn is a supply-side health indicator.

**GMV per active seller per month.** Total transaction value divided by active sellers. This tells us whether the platform is generating real income for sellers (not just facilitating low-value experiments).

What we **don't** track:
- Listing count (vanity — many listings with no sales is not healthy)
- User sign-up rate without subsequent activity
- Session duration (marketplace UX is often quick and transactional — long sessions can indicate confusion, not engagement)

## The Hyve's health metrics

**Messages per active workspace per day.** Communication volume indicates active use. Target: 30+ messages per workspace per day for teams using The Hyve as their primary communication tool.

**AI invocations per workspace per week.** The AI agent is The Hyve's differentiator. If workspaces aren't using it, they're paying for a Slack alternative, not an AI workspace tool. Target: 10+ AI invocations per workspace per week.

**Workspace retention at 90 days.** What fraction of workspaces that were active in month 1 are still active in month 3? For SaaS, 90-day retention is the leading indicator of long-term durability. Target: 60%+.

**NPS of active users.** Unlike Scoutr and The Marqet where success is transactional, The Hyve's value is ongoing. NPS measures whether active users would recommend the tool. Target: 40+ (SaaS tool benchmark).

## What we don't track

**Revenue per metric.** We know total revenue by product. We don't allocate revenue to individual marketing channels, user segments, or features. The data volume doesn't justify the tracking complexity.

**Funnel completion rates at each step.** We know overall conversion from registration to first transaction. We don't have a precise breakdown of where in the onboarding funnel users drop off. This is a known gap — we'll add more granular funnel tracking when the product is stable enough that the funnel isn't changing every sprint.

**Social engagement metrics.** How many people liked the latest tweet. Not informative about product health.

## The review cadence

Health metrics are reviewed weekly, in a consistent format. The format matters: the same metrics every week makes trends visible faster than ad hoc reviews. If a metric changes significantly in either direction, we investigate that week — not next quarter.

The review is short (30 minutes) and action-oriented: which metric changed, what's the likely cause, what do we do about it. The goal is not to have a metric-review meeting; it's to have a decision-making meeting that metrics inform.

## The honest constraint

We track these metrics because PostHog and Stripe make them available without significant instrumentation effort. The hardest health metric to track — "did the product actually help the user succeed?" — isn't a number we get from analytics. It comes from talking to users.

The metrics are proxies. The conversations are the ground truth.
