Pricing is one of those topics where the theoretical advice is abundant and the practical experience is scarce. Everyone says "charge more than you think", "don't undervalue your product", "price to value not to cost." The advice is correct and also unhelpful without the specifics.

Here's the Scoutr pricing story: what we set, what happened, and what we changed.

## The initial pricing

Scoutr launched at £9/month. We chose this number by looking at comparable tools in adjacent markets, cutting it in half ("we're new, we should be cheaper"), and rounding to a number that felt small.

This was a mistake, and not primarily for the reason people expect.

The problem wasn't that £9 was too low in absolute terms. The problem was that £9 communicated something incorrect about the product: that it was casual, that it was for hobbyists, that the value was modest.

Scoutr is a professional tool. The target user is someone doing Amazon arbitrage or retail arbitrage as a business, calculating fees and margins on products they're considering for resale. For that user, a wrong purchase decision costs them real money — not the cost of the tool, but the margin lost on products that don't perform. A tool that helps them make better decisions is worth the margin improvement on a handful of good decisions.

£9/month doesn't communicate "professional tool for business decisions." It communicates "another small app."

## The signal pricing sends

Pricing is information. Before a user knows anything about your product, the price tells them:

- Who you think the user is (consumer vs. professional)
- How much work went into it (low prices imply low investment)
- What problem it solves (high-value problem or nice-to-have?)
- Whether you stand behind it (confident companies charge confidently)

A price that's too low signals a low-confidence product regardless of the actual quality. We were filtering out professional users before they'd had a chance to evaluate the product, because the price told them it wasn't serious.

## What we changed

We moved Scoutr to a tiered structure:

**Starter: £15/month** — individual reseller, single marketplace, up to 100 scans/month
**Professional: £35/month** — multiple marketplaces, unlimited scans, batch processing, export
**Business: £79/month** — everything, plus priority support and API access

The new pricing repositioned Scoutr as a professional tool. The Professional tier is priced at the level a working reseller would pay for any business tool without deliberating — it's less than a month's worth of postage on a modest volume of sales.

The conversion rate from trial to paid improved after the price increase. This is the counterintuitive outcome that pricing advice predicts but that's hard to believe until you see it: higher price, higher conversion, because the price aligned with user expectations for a professional tool.

## The Marqet's pricing problem

The Marqet is a marketplace. Individual listing prices range from £3 to £25 depending on complexity. There's no subscription — it's transactional.

The challenge with transactional pricing in a content marketplace: the value of any individual listing is unknown until after purchase. Users are buying access to a specific AI configuration; they can't fully evaluate it from the description.

We've iterated on this through three mechanisms:

**Preview**: some listings include a reduced-function preview that gives users a sense of the output quality before purchasing. This increases conversion for listings that have it.

**Bundles**: professional role bundles (the "Marketing Professional Pack", the "Software Engineer Pack") include 6-8 relevant listings at a 30% discount. Users get more value per pound and we get higher transaction values.

**Refund policy**: 7-day no-questions refund on any listing. This reduces purchase hesitation. The refund rate is low (under 3%), confirming that the actual product quality meets the expectation set by listing descriptions.

## What we'd do differently

Price higher initially and offer a launch discount, rather than launching at a low price and raising it later. It's psychologically easier for users to see a discount than to see a price increase.

Test pricing before building the full product. A landing page with a pricing section and an "interested" CTA tells you whether the price is plausible before you've built anything. We skipped this step.

Think about what the price communicates, not just what it is in absolute terms. £9 and £35 are both "affordable" to a working professional. But they communicate very different things about the product.
