Marketplaces have a fundamental bootstrapping problem: buyers want a marketplace with sellers, sellers want a marketplace with buyers. The empty marketplace has nothing to offer either side.

The Marqet needed listings before it could attract buyers. Here's how we got from zero to 167.

## The strategy: supply-side first, single-creator

The standard marketplace playbook for bootstrapping supply: the founding team creates the initial supply manually, or recruits a small number of power sellers who create high-quality early inventory.

We chose the simpler version: we created the initial supply ourselves.

Before The Marqet launched publicly, we built out the first 50 listings across the professional categories we'd identified: software engineering, marketing, data and analytics, operations, strategy and consulting. These were the professional roles most likely to be early adopters of AI tools.

Creating 50 listings meant understanding what high-quality AI configurations looked like across different professional domains, testing them for the use cases they claimed, and writing listing descriptions that were specific enough to be useful.

This took time. It also gave us something more valuable than speed: we learned what a listing should be by building many of them before any external creator had.

## The initial 50 defined the quality bar

The listings we built established the implicit quality standard for the marketplace. When external creators eventually listed their configurations, they were benchmarking against the existing catalogue.

This was intentional. If the first listings on a marketplace are low-quality, the quality bar is low and difficult to raise. If the founders create a high-quality foundation, later creators see the bar and aim for it.

We also built the listing creation UI with the constraint that a listing required a specific structure: title, professional category, 200-word minimum description, at least 3 use cases, and a preview example. This forced listing quality at the structural level.

## The public launch with 50+ listings

We launched The Marqet publicly with 63 listings — the original 50 plus 13 created in the final two weeks before launch by a small group of early access users we'd invited.

63 listings was enough to make the browse experience useful. Not comprehensive, but useful. There were 8-12 listings in each major professional category, enough for a visitor to find something relevant.

The lesson from the launch: coverage matters more than depth at launch. Having 6 listings per category is more useful to a visitor than having 30 listings in two categories and nothing in the others.

## Growth to 167

Post-launch listing growth came from two sources:

**External creators.** The Marqet has an open listing process — anyone can list an AI configuration. We approved listings manually for the first two months (ensuring quality standards) and have since moved to automated approval with post-publication quality review.

**Our own expansions.** As we identified gaps in the catalogue through user search data and browsing patterns, we built listings to fill them. "Consulting and advisory" was a gap that user behavior revealed; we built 12 listings for that category.

The ratio today is roughly 40% our own listings, 60% external creator listings. The external creator percentage is growing.

## The demand-side: what made buyers come

Buyers came primarily through search and community. The Marqet's SEO advantage: 167 specific professional AI use cases, each with a dedicated page, creates a large surface area of long-tail search terms.

The community component: sharing listings in professional Slack groups, LinkedIn posts about specific AI use cases, and the journal has all contributed to discovery. Posts that discuss specific AI use cases by professional role attract people with those use cases.

## What we'd do differently

**Launch with more coverage.** We had 63 listings at launch across 6 major categories. The weakest launch categories (those with fewer than 8 listings) had noticeably worse conversion from browse to purchase. Getting to 10+ per category before launch would have helped.

**Build the creator program earlier.** We didn't have a documented path for external creators until two months after launch. Earlier investment in creator onboarding — documentation, support, quality guidelines — would have accelerated the external supply growth.

**Price anchoring.** Our initial listing prices were clustered in a narrow band (£5-12). Introducing premium listings (£20-25 for more complex, extensively documented configurations) earlier would have established a price range that makes the mid-range listings feel accessible rather than arbitrary.
