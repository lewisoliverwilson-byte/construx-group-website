200 dispatches. The first was in March 2026. It was about choosing a name for the company.

The last 199 have covered: database schemas, pricing psychology, real-time architecture, eBay taxonomy trees, trust signals, cold start strategies, TypeScript pipelines, job queues, SEO decisions, content moderation, dispute resolution, and the economics of dropshipping at scale.

It's a strange artefact. A public build log for three companies in progress, written during the same weeks the code was being shipped. The journal and the products evolved together.

## What surprised us

**The discipline effect.** Writing about what we built forced us to understand it more precisely than shipping it did. A blog post about the token bucket rate limiter required us to think through the edge cases clearly enough to explain them. Several design decisions improved because the act of writing exposed their weaknesses. The journal made the products better.

**The compounding effect.** Early dispatches attracted almost no readers. That was expected. But the readers who found the journal through later dispatches often read backwards through the archive — spending significant time with posts from months ago. Consistent publishing created a deep well for late arrivals. Post 47 still brings in readers in month 9.

**The credibility effect.** The number of times "I found you through your journal" appears in inbound contact is surprising. 200 detailed, honest posts about the technical and strategic decisions behind three products is a more credible sales document than any pitch deck. Readers who spent two hours in the journal arrive knowing more about how we work than most hiring managers know about candidates after an interview.

**The discipline of a public commitment.** We shipped 200 dispatches because they were public. On the days when a post felt like an interruption to actual building, the public commitment pulled us through. A private build log would have had 23 entries.

## What we still believe (that surprised us by holding)

**Small teams compound.** The theory was: two people with the right tools can move as fast as a larger team without the coordination overhead. 9 months in, this holds. The overhead of coordinating more than two people — meetings, alignment, context-switching, decision routing — is underestimated in startup culture, which has a fetish for team size as a proxy for seriousness.

**Shipping beats planning.** The Scoutr MVP went live 3 weeks after the idea was formed. It was incomplete. Several decisions made under time pressure turned out to be wrong. We fixed them. The feedback from real users in week 4 was worth more than another month of planning would have been. This is a cliché because it's true.

**Public build logs are underused.** The conventional wisdom is that building in public is risky — you might reveal a strategy a competitor will copy, or admit to weaknesses that investors will penalise. We haven't found this. The people who would copy a strategy without the capability to execute it aren't a threat. The investors who penalise honest operational disclosure aren't investors worth having.

## What changed

**Our view on pricing.** We started believing lower prices attract users. The data from The Marqet showed the opposite in premium service categories. We updated.

**Our view on AI as tooling.** We started treating AI as a capable junior developer that needed significant oversight. It's more capable than that. The better mental model: a very senior contractor who is broadly capable across domains and occasionally wrong in ways that require verification. Less oversight, more trust, more ambition in what we ask it to build.

**Our view on customer success.** We started treating CS as a cost — a function you minimise by building products that don't create problems. The users who engage most deeply with support (disputes, edge cases, unusual use cases) are often the most valuable power users. We've restructured to engage proactively with heavy users rather than routing them to self-serve as quickly as possible.

## The one question we're still sitting with

Can a company stay small intentionally and still be durable?

Small teams are faster, more coherent, and more aligned. They're also more fragile — key person risk is real, capacity is limited, and the failure modes of individual performance become failure modes of the company.

Every growth heuristic in the startup playbook assumes you're trying to get big. The metrics, the org design patterns, the advice — all of it assumes growth as a goal. There's much less thinking about how to be deliberately small, sustainable, and high-quality for a long time.

We don't have an answer. We have a hypothesis: the answer is margin. Businesses that are profitable enough to absorb shocks and reinvest in quality can resist the pressure to grow fast. Businesses operating at the edge of survival can't.

All three Construx ventures are built toward strong unit economics. Not profitability yet — we're still in growth mode. But the unit economics of each venture support the belief that the business can be profitable at moderate scale without requiring scale for survival.

Whether that translates to a deliberately small company that lasts a long time — we'll find out over the next 200 dispatches.
