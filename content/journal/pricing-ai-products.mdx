Pricing software is hard. You're setting a number that needs to cover your costs, feel fair to users, and capture enough of the value you create that the business works. Get it wrong and you either leave money on the table or kill adoption before the product can prove itself.

Pricing AI software is harder. The cost of running the product isn't fixed — it's a function of Claude API costs that have dropped dramatically since we started and will continue to drop. You're pricing into a future you can't fully predict.

Here's what we've learned.

## The misconception: AI = expensive

The first thing most people assume about AI products is that they're expensive to run. This is understandable — the early LLM days (2022-2023) had per-request costs that made many product ideas economically unviable.

That's no longer the case. Claude's API costs have dropped significantly, and continued drops are probable given the direction of model efficiency research. The cost structure for Scoutr today means our API costs per scan are a fraction of what they were when we planned the business.

This matters for pricing because it means the floor is lower than it looks, and the floor keeps moving.

## Two models: subscription vs usage

We've run both on our products.

**Subscription** (Scoutr, The Marqet): a fixed monthly fee regardless of how much the user uses the product. Predictable revenue, predictable costs (users self-regulate usage when they're paying a fixed fee). The risk is setting the price too low for heavy users who extract disproportionate value.

**Usage-based** (The Hyve, in credit form): users buy credits, spend credits as they use AI features. Directly proportional to cost — heavy users pay more. The risk is creating friction at the point of use: every action has a cost, which makes users hesitant to explore.

The hybrid model we've moved toward for The Hyve: a base subscription that includes a generous credit allowance, with additional credits available above that. Users who stay within the base allowance feel they're getting good value; heavy users can go further without us subsidising them.

## How we set the Scoutr price

The original Scoutr price was set wrong. Too low. We set it based on estimated API costs at the time, applied a margin, and ended up at a number that made the product feel low-value rather than accessible.

We raised the price after the first cohort. The counter-intuitive result: fewer trials, higher conversion on trials, lower churn. The raised price communicated that the product did something serious. Users who paid more used it more carefully and got more value from it.

The lesson: pricing is positioning. A price that feels too low doesn't attract bargain-hunters — it creates doubt about whether the product is worth using seriously.

## Pricing ahead of cost decreases

Here's the uncomfortable truth about pricing AI products: if you price based on today's API costs, you'll undercharge tomorrow.

As model costs drop, your margin improves — unless you've priced so aggressively that you've attracted a user base that will expect prices to fall as costs fall. Consumer expectations around "AI should be cheap" are already forming, driven partly by how products have been positioned.

Our approach: set prices based on the value delivered, not the cost to deliver. Scoutr's value is saving hours of manual margin calculation that a reseller would otherwise do themselves. That value is £15-30 in time saved per sitting. Our price reflects a fraction of that value — not our API cost.

As our API costs drop, our margin improves. We don't pass all of that to users unless competitive pressure requires it. We reinvest it in product improvements that increase the value delivered.

## The early-user discount question

Should you offer lower prices to early users as a reward for taking a chance on an unproven product? The standard answer is yes: early users take on product risk, they should get something in return.

The risk is that "early user pricing" becomes "permanent pricing" because raising prices on existing users is hard. We've seen products that locked in a cohort at a low price and couldn't raise it for years because of perceived unfairness.

Our approach: offer genuinely useful things to early users (direct access to founders, feature influence, recognition) rather than discounts. If you do offer lower pricing, make it explicitly time-limited and transition clearly to standard pricing when the window closes.

## The number that matters

The operational metric for AI product pricing isn't gross margin or ARPU in isolation — it's the ratio of value delivered to price charged.

If a user is getting £50 of time value from the product and paying £10, there's headroom to raise price. If they're getting £12 of value and paying £10, you have a product problem, not a pricing problem.

Track this. Ask users what they'd compare using Scoutr to doing manually. Quantify the time. Set your price relative to the value, not relative to your costs, and adjust as the product improves and the value increases.
