The hardest part of building a marketplace isn't the product. It's the chicken-and-egg problem: buyers won't come without sellers, and sellers won't come without buyers.

We knew this going in. We planned for it poorly.

## The original plan: build it and they'll come

The original go-to-market assumed that the quality of The Marqet would be sufficient to attract sellers organically. We'd launch, post about it, and professional eBay service providers would find us.

This was wrong. The professional eBay reseller community is concentrated in a handful of forums, Facebook groups, and YouTube channels. They discover new tools through community endorsement, not through organic search. A cold launch with no community presence produces zero traction.

We launched with 8 self-created listings (services we could actually provide ourselves or that we'd pre-recruited in the previous week) and had 3 organic seller sign-ups in the first two weeks. Not enough.

## The manual outreach phase

We spent 6 weeks doing manual outreach. The approach:

**Forums.** The UK eBay Sellers Megathread on Reddit, specialist Facebook groups for resellers, the eBay community boards. We'd identify people who were already providing services informally — answering questions in threads, offering to help with specific problems — and message them directly.

The pitch: we're building a professional marketplace for eBay services. The platform is free to join. We're recruiting the first cohort of sellers who'll have first-mover advantage and input into how the platform develops.

**The response rate.** About 40% of direct messages resulted in a response; about 20% of responses resulted in a listing. Worse than we expected, but not zero.

**What made people say yes:** being an early seller on a new platform with a plausible story and a professional-looking product. The fact that listing was free lowered the barrier significantly. The promise of early input into the platform was compelling to people who'd experienced frustrations with existing platforms.

**What made people say no:** time. Creating a good listing takes 30-60 minutes. For someone already running a business, this is a real cost. Several promising contacts declined because they didn't have the time, not because they didn't believe in the platform.

## The listing quality intervention

Early sellers created listings of widely varying quality. Some were excellent — detailed, professional, clearly describing the deliverable. Most were too vague ("I will help you with eBay" is not a service).

We couldn't launch with vague listings. Poor listing quality would hurt buyer conversion and reflect badly on the platform.

The intervention: manual listing quality reviews. Every listing from the first 40 sellers went through a review process. We'd read the listing, identify what was unclear, and write a specific email to the seller with improvement suggestions:

> "Your listing says you'll analyse product profitability. Could you add: how many products per analysis, what data you use (eBay completed sales? Keepa?), what format the output is in, and how quickly you'll deliver? Buyers want to know exactly what they're buying."

About 70% of sellers who received these emails improved their listings. 30% didn't respond. We eventually removed listings that hadn't been improved after two follow-up emails.

This was labour-intensive and didn't scale — hence the automated quality scoring pipeline we built later. But for the first 40 sellers, manual review produced better results than any automated system would have.

## The first 10 were the hardest

The conventional wisdom about two-sided marketplaces is true: the first 10 sellers are harder to get than the next 30. The first sellers have no social proof, no existing transaction history, no way to point to other sellers as validation.

From seller 11 onwards, we could point to seller 1-10. "We already have professional sellers doing X — here's an example." That reference is surprisingly powerful.

We reached seller 10 at week 8 post-launch. We reached seller 40 at week 14. The pace accelerated as the reference pool grew.

## The community strategy that actually worked

The tactic that worked best, retrospectively, was inviting specific sellers to be founding members of a Sellers Advisory group. Six sellers who'd created high-quality listings and been active on the platform were invited to a private Slack channel to give us feedback on marketplace direction.

This created three outcomes:
1. **Loyalty.** These sellers became advocates who'd mention The Marqet positively in community discussions.
2. **Referrals.** Three of the six introduced us to other professional service providers in their networks.
3. **Product insight.** Their feedback shaped several features we hadn't planned, including the quality classification pipeline.

The Sellers Advisory group cost us nothing except the time to respond to their messages. It produced a disproportionate return.

## Where we are now

167 listings, 42 active sellers. The seller pipeline is now mostly inbound — new sellers find the platform through existing sellers, community discussions, and the journal posts about The Marqet's architecture and approach. We still do occasional manual outreach for specific service categories we want better coverage in.

The marketplace is no longer supply-constrained. The current constraint is buyer growth — which is a different and (in some ways) easier problem to have.
