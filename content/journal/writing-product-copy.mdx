There's a recognisable texture to AI-generated marketing copy. Not because it's bad — technically it's usually fine. But because everyone is using the same tools with the same prompts, the result is a vocabulary that has become meaningless through overuse.

"Revolutionary." "Seamless." "Powerful." "Transform your workflow." "The future of [category]." "Designed for [persona]." 

If your copy sounds like this, it sounds like everyone else's copy. And copy that sounds like everyone else's means nothing to the reader — they've learned to skim past it.

## The problem with aspirational language

Marketing copy defaults to aspirational language because aspirational language is easy to write and hard to critique. "Powerful AI-driven insights" can't be wrong because it says nothing. There's no claim to verify, no promise to hold you to, no specific thing to believe or disbelieve.

Users have become very good at detecting and ignoring aspirational language. The moment they see "transform your workflow," their pattern-matching kicks in: this is marketing, not information. They start looking for the specific thing that would actually help them decide.

If your copy is all aspirational language, you're invisible to the reader who's actually evaluating your product.

## Specificity as credibility

The cure for aspirational copy is specificity. Specific claims are credible. Specific claims can be verified. Specific claims communicate more information in fewer words.

Compare:

"Powerful AI-driven product research that transforms how you find opportunities."

versus

"Scan any Amazon product page in under 5 seconds. Get the exact FBA fee breakdown, margin estimate, and competition density score."

The second version is shorter, more specific, and more useful. A reseller who does product research reads it and immediately knows whether this is relevant to them. The first version gives them nothing to evaluate.

Specificity comes from knowing your product well. You can't write specific copy without understanding exactly what the product does and for whom.

## Write for the reader who's already interested

Generic copy tries to appeal to everyone. Specific copy appeals to the right person.

The reader who's right for your product is already predisposed to find it compelling. They don't need to be convinced that your category matters — they know it does. What they need is evidence that your specific product is the right choice for their specific situation.

Scoutr's copy doesn't explain why Amazon reselling matters. It assumes the reader already knows. It talks about the things that matter to experienced resellers: fee accuracy, scan speed, the specific data points that affect margin calculation.

A reader who doesn't already care about Amazon margins isn't going to become a customer from reading any version of the landing page. We're not writing for them. We're writing for the person who already does this work and wants to know if Scoutr does it well.

## Proof over promise

Every claim in marketing copy is a promise. "The most accurate margin calculator" promises accuracy. "The fastest scan time in the category" promises speed. These promises set expectations the product has to meet.

Proof converts promises into facts. Instead of "the most accurate margin calculator," we show the fee breakdown for a real product: "We include Amazon referral fees (15%), FBA fulfillment (£2.41), FBA monthly storage (£0.14), and return processing. Most tools stop at referral fees."

This is a stronger claim and a more trustworthy one. It's not an assertion — it's a demonstration. The reader can verify it. They can compare it to the tools they currently use.

Proof is harder to write than assertions. It requires knowing your product well enough to demonstrate it, not just describe it.

## The founder voice

There's a specific voice that founder-written copy has: direct, opinionated, slightly defensive of the technical decisions made, proud of the specific things that are genuinely good.

This voice is harder to fake than to find. If you built the product, you know which technical decisions you're proud of and why. You know the specific problems you solved that others hadn't solved. You know the user feedback that confirms you're on to something.

Write from that knowledge. Don't translate it into marketing-speak. "We spent three weeks getting the FBA fee calculation exactly right because the existing tools were wrong by 15-20% on items over 300g" is a better sentence than "our industry-leading accuracy gives you confidence in your decisions."

## What to cut

If you're editing existing copy, here's what to look for:

Words that could describe any product in your category: powerful, intelligent, seamless, innovative, next-generation, cutting-edge. Cut them or replace them with the specific claim they're trying to make.

Promises without evidence: "the most accurate," "the fastest," "the best." Either add the evidence or change the claim to something you can actually demonstrate.

Passive voice about the user's experience: "users experience seamless integration." Replace with what actually happens: "paste your Amazon URL. Get results in 4 seconds. No account needed."

Hedged claims: "designed to help you potentially improve your workflow." If you don't believe in what the product does, the copy isn't the problem.

## The test

Read your copy as the most skeptical version of your target user. The person who has been burned by overpromising products before. The person who is looking for a reason not to trust you.

Does your copy give them specific things to evaluate? Does it make claims they can verify? Does it demonstrate the product rather than just asserting its value?

If the answer is no — if the copy is just a sequence of assertions without demonstration — rewrite it from the perspective of what the product actually does and why that's useful for a specific person in a specific situation.

The best product copy reads like a knowledgeable friend is explaining why this particular product is right for your particular problem. Not marketing. Not AI-generated filler. A recommendation from someone who knows.
