The Marqet is a two-sided marketplace. Buyers find service sellers. Sellers find buyers. Neither works without the other — and at launch you have neither.

This is the marketplace cold-start problem. It's a well-documented challenge. The documented solutions are mostly theoretical. Here's what we actually did to get to 50 active sellers.

## The strategy: supply first

The standard advice for marketplace cold starts is to solve one side first, and almost always supply is the right side to start with. An empty marketplace has nothing for buyers to browse — even if they arrive, they leave immediately. A supply-rich marketplace gives you something to show buyers and something to talk about in discovery.

We chose supply first. Goal: 50 active service sellers with at least one live listing before investing in demand generation.

## Phase 1: direct outreach (0-15 sellers)

Before The Marqet had a listing interface, we identified the sellers we wanted.

The ideal seller for The Marqet's launch: a freelancer with documented AI skills, currently selling services on a general-purpose platform (Upwork, Fiverr, Contra), and underpriced relative to the value they're delivering.

We found candidates by:
1. Searching Upwork and Fiverr for "Claude", "prompt engineering", "AI assistant", "AI workflow"
2. Looking at LinkedIn for freelancers who'd recently updated their profiles with AI skills
3. Following accounts on X/Twitter who posted about their freelance AI work

We reached out directly with a specific offer: a founder waitlist spot on a new platform launching in 4 weeks, focused specifically on AI services, with zero platform fees for the first 6 months.

The message was short and specific:

> I'm building a marketplace for AI professionals — specifically people doing the work you do. We're launching in 4 weeks. For the first 50 sellers, zero platform fees for 6 months, and you get first-mover positioning in your category. Reply here if you want the details.

Of 87 outreach messages, 31 responded positively, 18 proceeded to create accounts, and 15 listed at least one service.

The failure rate (87 → 15) is not a problem — it's a filter. The 15 who listed were serious enough to go through the steps.

## Phase 2: inbound from journal traffic (15-35 sellers)

After Phase 1, we wrote a dispatch about building The Marqet's listing system (dispatch #42) and two posts about how the pricing worked for sellers.

Those posts brought inbound interest. People who found the content came to the site, read the seller proposition, and signed up. Not many — 4-5 per week — but consistently and without outreach effort.

Inbound sellers from the journal were higher quality than outreach sellers: they'd read enough about how we think to be pre-qualified. They asked better questions, set up better listings, and were more likely to respond to feedback.

## Phase 3: seller referrals (35-50 sellers)

At around 35 sellers, we started seeing referrals. Sellers who were happy with the platform mentioned it to their network. Freelancers who knew each other would both show up on the platform without either having been directly recruited.

We accelerated this with a referral incentive: sellers who brought in another seller got a 3-month fee waiver extension. Small incentive, but it made the referral mechanism explicit.

10 of our 35-50 sellers came from referrals.

## What didn't work

**Paid promotion.** We ran £200 of paid social for one week. Drove 140 clicks to the seller landing page. Zero sign-ups. The targeting wasn't precise enough — general "freelancer" audiences weren't specifically looking for an AI marketplace.

**Cold email to agencies.** Reaching out to established AI consulting agencies to list their associates as individual sellers. They weren't interested — their margin structure depends on their own brand, not a third-party platform.

**Press/media outreach.** We pitched to tech journalists. One responded, didn't write anything. We spent more time on the pitch than it was worth.

## The seller quality gradient

Not all sellers are equal. Our 50 sellers divided roughly into:

- 10 high-quality sellers: excellent listings, competitive pricing, responsive to inquiries, have successfully completed transactions
- 25 adequate sellers: decent listings, occasional responsiveness, a few transactions
- 15 inactive or low-quality: signed up, listed something, haven't engaged since

This gradient is normal for marketplaces. The 10 high-quality sellers generate most of the GMV and almost all of the positive buyer experiences. We invested disproportionate attention in this group — direct messaging, featured placement, handling issues immediately.

## The supply number that unlocks demand

We targeted 50 sellers before investing in buyer acquisition. In retrospect, 50 was the right threshold. Below that, the marketplace felt sparse; buyers who arrived didn't find enough variety. At 50+, the density was sufficient to justify targeted buyer acquisition.

The number that "unlocks demand" varies by marketplace. It's the point where a buyer searching your category is likely to find at least 3-5 relevant options. For The Marqet's category structure, that was 50 sellers across 4 categories — roughly 12 per category.

## What we'd do differently

**More filtering upfront.** We accepted some sellers who weren't a good fit (general "AI chatbot" listings with no specialisation). A clearer seller criteria document would have saved the moderation time.

**Community before platform.** A private Slack or Discord group for "AI services freelancers" before the marketplace existed would have built a committed supply base. The platform launch becomes an announcement to an existing community, not a cold start.

**Video demos.** Sellers who posted a short video demo of their service converted buyers at higher rates. We should have made video onboarding a requirement, not optional.

The 50-seller milestone took 8 weeks. Looking back, the playbook was right: supply first, direct outreach to high-quality candidates, journal content for inbound, referrals for compounding.
