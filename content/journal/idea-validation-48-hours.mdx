We've run the idea validation process four times. Three times led to products we built. Once led to a clear "no" that saved us from building something that wouldn't have worked.

Here's the process.

## The premise

Most new product ideas fail not because they're badly executed but because they solve a problem that's either not real, not acute, or not paid for. Validation at the idea stage is about answering: is this a real problem, is it painful enough that people will pay to solve it, and is there a viable path to reaching those people?

48 hours is enough to get directional answers to all three questions. The goal is not certainty — it's to identify obvious red flags before committing weeks of development time.

## The validation checklist

**Hour 1-4: Problem clarity**

Write one paragraph that describes the problem in concrete terms. Not "people struggle with X" but "a specific person, in a specific context, experiences a specific friction, with a specific consequence."

For Scoutr, the problem statement: "Amazon and eBay resellers who source products from retail sites (retail arbitrage) spend 20-40 minutes per product manually calculating whether it will be profitable to resell after all fees. They do this calculation in spreadsheets. They make mistakes. They buy products that turn out to be unprofitable. They also miss profitable products because the calculation takes too long."

If you can't write this paragraph, the problem is not clear enough to build for.

**Hour 4-8: Existing alternatives research**

What are people currently doing instead? This reveals:
- The real competition (not "similar tools" but what people actually use)
- The acceptable solution bar (what the current solution is, so you know what "better" means)
- The market's willingness to accept change (if the current solution is embedded in workflows, switching costs are high)

For Scoutr: current solution is a combination of mental arithmetic, spreadsheets, and a few partial tools like Keepa (which tracks prices but doesn't calculate resale fees). The spreadsheet solution is slow and error-prone. The partial tools don't complete the workflow.

**Hour 8-12: Market size estimation**

Not a TAM/SAM/SOM analysis. A rough answer to: is there a plausible path to 1000 paying users?

For Scoutr: Amazon has millions of sellers. A subset are retail arbitrage resellers (estimates range from 50,000 to 500,000 active resellers in the UK and US markets we target). If 0.2% of those become paying Scoutr users, that's 100-1000 users. Plausible.

If the answer is "less than 1000 plausible paying users", the idea has a ceiling problem.

**Hour 12-20: The AI differentiator question**

What does AI specifically enable that makes this product meaningfully better than a non-AI solution?

This is the Construx-specific test. If the answer is "AI makes it faster" or "AI generates text", those are weak differentiators. If the answer is "AI enables a capability that wasn't previously possible" — that's the threshold we want.

For Scoutr: AI enables accurate product classification across thousands of product types without hand-crafted rules. A rule-based fee calculator would require maintaining category-specific fee tables manually; Claude classifies products and applies the right fee structure dynamically.

For the ideas we rejected: the job tracker (AI fills in application forms) — the AI differentiator is weak (form filling with AI is table stakes). The accounting tool — the AI differentiator is "AI categorises expenses," which is already built by incumbents.

**Hour 20-36: Landing page and community test**

Build a minimal landing page (we use a simple static site or even a Notion page) that describes the problem and the proposed solution, and post about it in communities where the target user is active.

We're not measuring signups. We're measuring the quality and quantity of engagement. Does the post generate comments that demonstrate people recognise the problem? Do people ask "when will this be available?" or "I've been looking for exactly this"?

For Scoutr: posted in Amazon FBA and retail arbitrage subreddits. Got 40+ comments from resellers confirming the problem and asking for access. One person offered to pay immediately. This was the go signal.

For the idea we killed: the job tracker. Posted in job search communities. Got friendly responses but no urgency. "That would be nice eventually" — not "I need this now."

**Hour 36-48: The build complexity estimate**

A rough estimate of what it takes to build a minimum viable version. Not a full spec — just an answer to: can two people build something testable in 4 weeks?

If the MVP requires more than 4 weeks of full-time development, it's probably too complex for our first version. We simplify or deprioritize.

## The go/no-go criteria

Go: clear problem statement, existing alternatives that are painful or incomplete, plausible path to 1000 users, genuine AI differentiator, community evidence that the problem is real, MVP buildable in 4 weeks.

No-go: any of: problem is unclear or synthetic, existing alternatives are good enough, market ceiling is low, AI is window dressing, community response is polite but unenthusiastic.

## What validation doesn't tell you

Whether the product will succeed. Validation is a filter for obvious failures, not a predictor of success. A product idea that passes all validation criteria can still fail to find product-market fit.

The validated ideas fail for reasons validation can't catch: the go-to-market approach is wrong, the pricing doesn't work, the timing is off, the product quality isn't high enough. These are discovered in the build and launch, not in the 48-hour research phase.

Validation is the table stakes. The actual building still has to work.
