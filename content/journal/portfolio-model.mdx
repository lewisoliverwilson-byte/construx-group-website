The conventional startup advice is: find one problem, build one solution, go all-in, don't get distracted. This advice is generally good. It's also not what we're doing.

Here is the honest case for running multiple ventures simultaneously.

## What changed

The reason the "one bet" advice exists is largely economic. Building a product used to require significant time, capital, and headcount. If you have 18 months of runway and a team to pay for, spreading your attention across three bets doesn't work. You need the whole team pointed at the same target.

The economics shifted. A two-person team with the current state of AI tooling can build and ship a production product in weeks rather than months. The marginal cost of starting a new venture is dramatically lower than it was two years ago.

When the cost of a new bet drops below a certain threshold, the "go all-in" logic weakens. You can now run multiple experiments for roughly the same cost of running one.

## The compounding problem with single bets

There's something the conventional advice understates: the risk of being wrong about your one bet.

Most founders who go all-in on a single product are making a bet about three things simultaneously: that the problem is real, that their solution is the right one, and that they can build it before running out of time and money. Getting all three wrong is catastrophic. Getting one wrong is often fatal.

The portfolio model hedges differently. Each venture is an independent experiment. Scoutr is a bet that resellers need AI-powered margin intelligence. The Marqet is a bet that there's demand for professional Claude configurations as a product category. The Hyve is a bet that AI-first teams need a workspace built natively around an AI team member.

These bets are independent. If Scoutr is wrong, it doesn't invalidate The Marqet. If The Marqet is wrong, The Hyve might still be right. Getting one right out of three is a viable outcome. Getting all three right is obviously better, but the downside scenario is more survivable.

## What it actually costs

I want to be honest about the trade-offs.

Running three ventures means none of them gets your undivided attention. Some things move slower than they would if we were a single-product studio. Features that take a week for a focused team might take three weeks when shared across three products. Support quality is lower than it would be if we had a dedicated person on each product.

There is also cognitive overhead. Switching contexts between three products requires discipline. Getting deep on one problem and then pulling yourself out to address something on a different product is harder than staying in one context indefinitely.

The honest answer to "what does it cost" is: it costs focus. And focus is genuinely valuable. If any one of our ventures turned out to be a runaway product that needed everything we had, we'd need to make a choice. We haven't faced that choice yet.

## Why now is the right time

This model is specifically enabled by the current state of AI tooling. The costs I'm describing — context switching, slower feature velocity, divided attention — are significantly offset by the speed at which AI lets a small team execute.

We can build a new feature on Scoutr in a morning, respond to The Marqet user feedback in the afternoon, and still have time to review a design decision on The Hyve in the evening. That's not because we're unusually fast — it's because the current tools collapse the implementation gap between having an idea and having a working thing to react to.

Without Claude, without Claude Code, without the AI-first build process we've documented in this journal, the portfolio model probably wouldn't work at this scale. The speed that AI gives us is what makes distributed attention viable.

## The honest unknown

What we don't know yet: does any of this scale?

Everything we're describing works at the scale we're currently operating — two founders, three live ventures, early revenue across a few of them. Whether the portfolio model still works at three founders, six ventures, or significant revenue-per-product, we don't know.

We think it does. The AI-first multiplier should increase as the tools improve, not decrease. But we haven't proven it yet at scale.

That's what this journal is for. The record, not the retrospective.
