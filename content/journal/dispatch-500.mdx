500 dispatches.

The first one shipped in May 2026. This is 500th. That's roughly one every three days across two and a half years, never missing a cadence. No ghostwriter, no content calendar, no SEO strategy. Just showing up and writing about what we were building.

Here's what that compounding looks like.

## The numbers

- **500 dispatches** published
- **Roughly 1,800 words per dispatch** average
- **900,000 words** total — about nine novels
- **3,000 hours** of writing time estimated
- **0 posts** we'd retract or be embarrassed by today

The technical accuracy has improved over time but the philosophy hasn't changed: write about what you're actually building. No tutorials for traffic. No hot takes for engagement. Just the honest record.

## What it became

When we started, the journal was a build log. A place to put the reasoning behind decisions so we could refer to it later. It still is that. But it's also become the most consistent signal we send to the world.

Every hire we've ever made came through someone who read the journal first. Every client engagement started with them reading six to ten dispatches before they ever contacted us. The journal doesn't generate leads in the traditional sense — it filters for exactly the kind of person we want to work with.

It also became a forcing function. Writing about architecture decisions forces you to be precise about what you actually decided and why. Writing about product reasoning forces you to interrogate whether the reasoning is actually sound. The act of writing forces clarity. The 500 posts made us better at building in ways that didn't come from the writing itself, but from the discipline the writing required.

## What we write

The rough split across 500 dispatches:

- **~60% Build Log** — technical implementation: the code, the libraries, the patterns
- **~20% Strategy** — how we think about product, market, and company
- **~15% Product** — what we're building and why
- **~5% Methodology** — how we work as a team

The Build Log posts have the best longevity. A dispatch on tRPC subscriptions or Postgres row-level security is useful to anyone who encounters that problem, regardless of when they find it. The strategy posts are more ephemeral — interesting as a record of how we thought at a given time, but less useful as evergreen references.

## What didn't compound

We tried adding dispatch length. Going from 800 words to 2,000 words per post didn't proportionally increase impact. The posts that got the most engagement were often the tightest ones: a clear problem, a clear solution, working code. Longer posts sometimes had more depth but didn't reach further.

We tried writing more frequently — five posts a week for a period. It burned the quality. The three-day cadence turned out to be the right pace: long enough to build something worth writing about, short enough to stay consistent.

## What surprised us

The most-read dispatches are rarely the ones we expected. The posts on environment variable validation with Zod, on database connection pooling, on the expand-contract migration pattern — those get found years after they were written by people encountering exactly that problem. The journal is a long-tail asset. Most of its value is in the aggregate, not any individual post.

The second surprise: writing 500 technical posts made us faster at building. The discipline of writing forces you to be precise. Precision forces you to understand things you might otherwise leave half-understood. We build better because we write about what we build.

## Dispatch 600

The journal continues. Dispatch 600 ships in roughly one year at the current cadence. By then we expect to have shipped two additional ventures, crossed some thresholds we haven't yet reached, and had enough new problems to write 100 more honest posts about.

The signal is live.
