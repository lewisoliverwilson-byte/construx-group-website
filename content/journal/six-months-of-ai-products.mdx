We started building AI-first products in earnest in early 2026. Six months and three live products later, some of the assumptions we started with have held, some have been corrected, and a few turned out to be completely wrong.

Here's the honest retrospective.

## What we overestimated

**The technical barrier.** We assumed building AI products would be harder than it turned out to be. The Claude API is well-designed. The patterns for streaming responses, structured output, and context management are learnable in days, not months. The technical execution of "an AI that does X" is rarely the limiting factor.

The limiting factor is almost always the same thing that limits non-AI products: finding the right problem, reaching the right users, and designing something they want to use repeatedly. The AI part doesn't change this.

**The cost of mistakes.** We expected to spend more time dealing with AI failures — wrong outputs, hallucinations, misclassifications — than we actually have. In production, Claude's error rate on well-specified tasks is low. The failures we've encountered are almost always at the edges of the specified task, where the input was ambiguous or the prompt didn't cover the case.

The lesson: precise prompts produce reliable output. The cost of investment in the prompt is lower than the cost of dealing with inconsistent output.

**User sophistication.** We expected users to understand that AI output is probabilistic and might need verification. Most don't — at least not initially. Users who've used ChatGPT have a model of "ask question, get answer" that doesn't include "verify this before acting on it." Setting up appropriate confidence calibration in the UI turned out to be more work than we expected.

## What we underestimated

**The value of being specific.** Each of our products succeeds by being very specific about one thing. Scoutr: profit margins for resellers. The Marqet: Claude configurations for specific professional roles. The Hyve: collaborative workspace for AI-first builder teams.

We expected specificity to limit audience size. It does — but it also makes the product dramatically easier to explain, sell, and improve. Users who know exactly what a product does for them are more likely to use it and more likely to recommend it.

**The operational overhead.** Running three live products with two people requires more active attention than we planned. Not emergencies — the products are stable — but the ongoing maintenance: monitoring outputs, checking error rates, responding to user questions, keeping the AI integrations working as the underlying models update.

We've gotten more systematic about this (the live product operations post covers the process in detail), but the overhead was higher than our initial estimate.

**The pace of model improvement.** Claude's capabilities in February were meaningfully different from Claude's capabilities in June. Outputs that required careful prompting in February now work with minimal prompting. Fee calculations that required structured output instructions now work with lighter-touch prompts.

This is a tailwind — our products get better as the model improves without us doing anything. But it also means prompts need periodic review: instructions written to compensate for a past model limitation might be unnecessary or even counterproductive on the current model.

## What turned out to be more true than we expected

**The professional user insight.** We expected our products to serve a mix of casual and professional users. The professional users — Scoutr's arbitrage traders, The Hyve's builder teams — have turned out to be the most engaged and the most valuable segment by far. Designing for serious users rather than casual ones was the right call.

**Build in public.** We wrote about our products, our decisions, our failures. This turned out to generate more early user interest than any other channel. People want to use products from builders who can explain what they're doing and why.

The journal you're reading is the most effective acquisition channel we have that doesn't cost money.

**Two founders is enough.** We questioned at various points whether we needed to hire. We still don't. Two people with the right tools (Claude Code, Amplify, the full modern JS stack) can ship and maintain three live SaaS products. The velocity isn't limited by headcount — it's limited by the quality of our decisions about what to build.

## What we'd do differently

**Start with the professional user.** Every product we've built has found its strongest use case in professional users doing serious work. We designed for casual users first and pivoted to professionals when the usage data showed us who was actually getting value. Starting with professionals would have saved months of redesign.

**Write decision logs from day one.** We built the decision engine in The Hyve because we noticed the problem — relitigating old decisions — after we'd lost the context for dozens of past choices. The decision log we keep now is one of the most valuable artefacts we have. We wish we'd started it earlier.

**Don't underinvest in copy.** Early versions of our products had vague, feature-list copy. Users couldn't tell what the product actually did or whether it would work for their specific situation. Good copy is product work, not marketing work. It takes the same care as the feature itself.

## The six-month thesis check

Before we started, our thesis was: small teams operating with AI can build and maintain multiple SaaS products that would previously have required larger teams.

Six months in, this thesis holds. Two founders, three live products, real users, real revenue. The AI leverage is real — and it's not just about writing code faster. It's about being able to maintain context across multiple products, operate with professional-grade tooling, and ship at a pace that wasn't previously possible at our size.

What we didn't anticipate: the compounding nature of the learning. Each product makes us better at the next one. The patterns, the mistakes, the user insights — they accumulate. The sixth month is more productive than the first, not just because we have more code, but because we understand the problem space better.
