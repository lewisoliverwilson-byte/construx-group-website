The Hyve launched as a single-user AI workspace. The core thesis: one person, with AI as a collaborator, can do the work that previously required a team. That's still the core thesis.

But teams exist. And teams using a product where AI is a team member have coordination problems that single-user products don't: who said what to the AI, what did the AI know when it said that, how do multiple people share a context with an AI agent.

Here's how we built The Hyve's team collaboration layer.

## The core problem

When multiple people interact with the same AI agent, the agent's context becomes a shared resource. User A tells the agent "we've decided to launch in Q3." User B doesn't know this was said. User B asks the agent about launch timing. The agent answers based on a fact that User B didn't know was established.

In human teams, this is partially solved by communication norms — people share decisions in channels, keep documentation updated, have meetings. In AI-augmented teams, it's more complex because the AI holds information that may not have been explicitly communicated to the human team.

The Hyve's team mode needed to solve this: make the AI's knowledge visible and shared, while preserving the conversational feel of individual AI interactions.

## Decision log as shared state

The architectural decision: The Hyve maintains a team decision log that any team member can write to, and that the AI reads from when generating responses.

When a team member has a conversation with the AI that reaches a decision ("we're targeting UK resellers first, not US"), either party can write that to the decision log. The AI suggests it: "should I add 'targeting UK resellers first' to the team decision log?" The user confirms or edits before it's added.

The decision log is visible to all team members. When any team member asks the AI a question that touches on logged decisions, the AI cites the log: "Based on the decision made on [date], we're targeting UK resellers first."

This makes the AI's knowledge explicit and traceable. Team members can see what the AI knows, who established it, and when. The AI can't hold a fact silently.

## Channel architecture

The Hyve organises AI interactions into channels, similar to Slack. Each channel has a distinct purpose and a distinct context window:

- `#product` — product decisions, feature specs, roadmap conversations
- `#research` — market research, competitor analysis, customer interviews
- `#ops` — operational decisions, process documentation, vendor management

The AI agent's context in each channel is scoped to that channel's history plus the shared decision log. This prevents context bleed between workstreams — a technical conversation in `#ops` doesn't pollute the AI's product context in `#product`.

Channel scoping also makes it easier for team members to find past AI conversations. Rather than scrolling through one undifferentiated history, conversations are organised by workstream.

## Permissions and attribution

Team mode introduces permission questions that single-user mode doesn't have. Who can write to the decision log? Who can delete decisions? Who can access which channels?

We chose a simple model: workspace admins (typically the founders) can write and delete from the decision log and access all channels. Team members can write to channels they're assigned to but can't delete decisions once logged.

The attribution question was important for accountability: every decision in the log shows which human team member approved it (not just which AI conversation generated it). The AI can suggest, but a human must confirm. This keeps humans in the accountability chain even as AI does more of the analytical work.

## Async AI interactions

One of the more interesting product decisions: whether the AI's response to a channel message is immediate (synchronous) or queued (asynchronous).

For individual use, synchronous responses are always right. You ask, the AI answers, you read the answer.

For team use, asynchronous is sometimes better. If User A posts a research question at 9am, User B might want to see both the question and the AI's response when they log in at 10am. A synchronous response that User B missed is equivalent to a synchronous response that User B ignored.

We implemented optional async mode for channel interactions: the AI drafts a response immediately but marks it "pending review" until a team member (or the person who asked the question) marks it as reviewed. This creates a lightweight review workflow for AI output before it influences the team.

## The complexity of shared AI context

The hardest technical problem in team mode: managing a shared AI context across multiple simultaneous users without creating race conditions or incoherent state.

If User A and User B are both in `#product` at the same time, sending messages in parallel, the AI sees interleaved messages that may be responding to different things. The conversation thread becomes ambiguous.

Our solution: conversations are threaded. Each message in a channel can start a thread. The AI's context for a response is the thread it's responding to, not the entire channel. Cross-thread references (when User A's thread is relevant to User B's question) are handled by the decision log — the output of one thread that matters for the whole team goes into the log, which all threads can reference.

This is a departure from how Slack works (messages mostly flow in-channel), but it makes the AI's context coherent at the cost of some conversation friction.

## What we shipped

The initial team mode shipped with:
- Channels (up to 5 per workspace, configurable)
- Shared decision log with human approval gate
- Thread-scoped AI context
- Basic attribution (who said what, who approved which decisions)
- Admin/member role distinction

What we didn't ship in v1:
- Guest access (planned for v2)
- Channel-level permission configuration
- AI-generated meeting summaries (the AI reads a meeting transcript, extracts decisions, proposes decision log entries)

The meeting summary feature is the one we're most excited about. It closes the loop between the human team's offline discussions and the AI's shared context — the place where most AI context gaps currently live.

Building team features for AI products is harder than building them for conventional SaaS. The shared resource isn't just data — it's the AI's understanding of the project. Making that understanding visible, attributable, and controllable is a product problem as much as a technical one.
