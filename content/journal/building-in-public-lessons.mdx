Building in public has been one of the most consistent pieces of advice in the startup space for the last decade. Share your journey. Narrate your progress. Be vulnerable about failures. Grow an audience that becomes customers.

We've done it — the journal you're reading is the artifact. Here's an honest account of what it actually produces.

## What it does

**It produces qualified inbound interest.** The people who find us through the journal are people who read detailed build logs and product thinking. They're technically literate, thoughtful about tools, and already interested in what we're building. The conversion rate from journal reader to trial user is higher than from any other channel, because the self-selection is so strong.

When someone has read a post about how Scoutr calculates margins, they arrive at the product with an accurate expectation. They know what they're going to get. This reduces the activation friction significantly — they're not discovering the product, they're confirming it.

**It creates accountability.** Writing down what we're working on, what's working, and what we're struggling with creates a record we're accountable to. We can't quietly change direction without the record showing it. This is a discipline, not just a marketing tool.

**It forces clear thinking.** If you can't write a coherent post about a decision you made, you probably haven't thought it through clearly. The constraint of having to explain why we made a decision in writing has improved the quality of our decision-making, not just the quality of the documentation.

**It builds trust over time, not quickly.** A single post doesn't build trust. Fifty posts over six months, documenting consistent thinking and real outcomes, does. The trust building is slow and compounds. The first few posts feel like they're generating nothing; by post 20, people are reaching out who found us from post 3.

## What it doesn't do

**It doesn't go viral.** "Going viral" from build logs requires either a dramatic narrative (failure, comeback, pivot) or a subject that touches a large existing audience. Our posts don't have either quality. They go to readers who care about the specific subjects, not to the general internet.

We've accepted this. The value isn't in viral distribution — it's in reaching exactly the right people. A post that reaches 300 people who care about AI product development is more valuable than a post that reaches 3,000 people who don't.

**It doesn't replace doing the work.** There's a failure mode in building in public where the narration substitutes for progress. Posting about what you're going to build, talking about your framework for making decisions, documenting your systems — none of this creates product value.

We've been strict about this: posts are written about work that's already done, not about work we're planning. The journal is a record, not a pitch.

**It doesn't produce short-term results.** If you need customers in the next thirty days, building in public won't help. The compounding nature of the trust-building means it's a six-month play at minimum. We started writing in the expectation that the payoff would come later.

## What surprises us

**The depth of engagement is unexpected.** People who reach out after reading the journal often reference specific posts and specific points. They've read carefully. The engagement quality is higher than anything we've experienced with conventional marketing.

**The posts that resonate aren't the ones we expected.** The posts that get the most response are usually the honest ones — about failure modes, about what we'd do differently, about the limitations of our approach. The polished "here's how we built this" posts underperform compared to "here's where we got this wrong."

**The journal builds the brand better than the brand page does.** A ventures page and a founders page tell people what we are. The journal shows people how we think. Showing is more persuasive than telling, especially for technical audiences who evaluate people on their thinking, not their marketing.

## The right expectation

Building in public is a long-term trust-building exercise that produces qualified interest from people who've already evaluated you through your writing. It's not audience growth, it's not viral marketing, and it's not a shortcut to customers.

For businesses where trust is the primary barrier to purchase (high-consideration tools, B2B products, products for technical users), the payoff is real. For businesses where the purchase decision is quick and emotional, it's probably not the highest-return channel.

We're building tools for professional users who evaluate tools carefully. Building in public is the right channel for this audience. It would be the wrong channel for a consumer product where the purchase decision is made in 10 seconds.

Know your audience. Know what builds trust with them. Write for that.
