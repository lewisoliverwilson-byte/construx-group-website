Building three products without external investment requires income from somewhere. The funding question is: consulting/freelance work or savings. We've been doing both.

Here's the honest picture.

## The consulting model

We take on freelance and consulting engagements from clients who need senior technical work: technical architecture reviews, production debugging, Next.js and TypeScript development, AI integration consulting. These are time-bounded engagements that generate revenue now.

The advantages:

**Immediate revenue.** No fundraising, no investor relationships, no equity dilution. The work generates revenue directly.

**Skills maintenance.** Working on real client problems keeps technical skills sharp in ways that building products alone doesn't. A debugging engagement where you have to understand someone else's codebase quickly, under pressure, in an unfamiliar context — this is different from working in your own codebase.

**Network.** Client relationships produce referrals, both for consulting and for the products. A client who sees you build something well is a likely early adopter for the products.

**Independence.** We can make any product decision without consulting an investor, board, or anyone else.

The disadvantages:

**Time.** Consulting time is not product time. There's a real tradeoff: an hour billed to a client is an hour not building.

**Context switching.** Moving between client work and product work requires mental context switching. We mitigate this by batching client work in specific weeks.

**Not infinitely scalable.** Consulting revenue scales with hours worked. Products, if they work, scale without proportional time investment.

## The model in practice

Currently: roughly 2-3 days per week on client work, 2-3 days per week on the products. The products are generating some revenue (Scoutr subscriptions, The Marqet transaction fees) but not enough to replace consulting income.

The trajectory: as product revenue grows, we shift more time from consulting to product. This has been happening gradually over the past year.

The target: product revenue sufficient to eliminate consulting work entirely. We're not there yet.

## Why this model is underrated

The narrative around founding startups emphasises raising money: pre-seed, seed, Series A. The consulting/freelance model is less glamorous and rarely discussed.

The advantages of not raising:

**No artificial deadline.** VC-backed companies operate on investor time horizons: show significant traction before the funding runs out or raise another round. The consulting model allows building at the right pace without running out of runway.

**No pivot pressure.** Investors want returns within a defined time horizon. This can pressure companies to abandon product bets that need more time. Without that pressure, you can build slower and more deliberately.

**All upside.** If the products become valuable, the equity is entirely ours. This is a powerful incentive to build things that last.

The disadvantage: bootstrapped companies grow more slowly than funded ones. If the market is moving fast and speed-to-market is critical, funding provides the runway to hire faster and move faster.

For our market (professional tools, marketplace, team collaboration) — these are not winner-take-all markets where being six months late is fatal. The deliberate pace is a reasonable tradeoff for the independence.

## The honest uncertainty

We don't know if this is the right model for us. An alternative: raise a seed round, hire, move faster. We might have more users, more revenue, and more market coverage if we'd done that.

What we do know: the products are live, they're generating revenue, and we haven't had to make a decision based on investor preferences rather than user preferences. Whether that advantage outweighs the speed disadvantage of bootstrapping is something we'll be able to evaluate more clearly as the products mature.

For now, the consulting model works. It funds the product work. And it keeps us independent.
