Every scan in Scoutr produces three outputs: a margin calculation, a sell-through estimate, and a confidence score. The margin and sell-through are factual calculations from data we retrieved. The confidence score is a meta-output: how much should the user trust those calculations?

The confidence score is shown as a 0-1 value (displayed as a percentage) alongside the margin data. A scan with 0.9 confidence means we have strong data; 0.3 means the numbers are speculative.

## What makes a scan uncertain

Multiple sources of uncertainty compound:

**Listing match quality.** Scoutr compares a source product URL to resale marketplace listings. If we find an exact match (same ASIN, same EAN), confidence is high. If we find a likely match based on title similarity and category, confidence drops. If we infer the likely resale format from category patterns without a direct match, confidence is low.

**Price sample size.** Sold listings data on eBay is the primary source for sell-through price estimation. A product with 50 sold listings in the past 30 days gives us strong price signal. A product with 3 sold listings gives us weak signal — the variance is too high.

**Data freshness.** Marketplace listing prices change rapidly. Data older than 6 hours is penalised. Data older than 24 hours is significantly penalised.

**Source price reliability.** Amazon's listed price is reliable. A third-party seller's price is less reliable (it may include inflated shipping, may be from a seller who rarely ships). Ebay Buy It Now prices are reliable; auction ending prices are variable.

**Category familiarity.** Scoutr has better models for some categories than others. Electronics and collectibles have deep data; obscure hobby goods have sparse data. Lower-confidence categories penalise the overall score.

## The algorithm

```ts
interface ScanInputs {
  listingMatchType: 'exact' | 'fuzzy' | 'inferred';
  soldCount30Days: number;
  dataStalenessHours: number;
  sourcePriceType: 'amazon' | 'ebay_bin' | 'ebay_auction' | 'other';
  categoryFamiliarity: number; // 0-1, precomputed per category
}

function calculateConfidence(inputs: ScanInputs): number {
  // Component scores (each 0-1)
  const matchScore = {
    exact: 1.0,
    fuzzy: 0.65,
    inferred: 0.3,
  }[inputs.listingMatchType];

  const sampleScore = Math.min(1, inputs.soldCount30Days / 20);
  // 0 sales → 0, 20+ sales → 1.0, linear between

  const freshnessScore = inputs.dataStalenessHours <= 6
    ? 1.0
    : inputs.dataStalenessHours <= 24
      ? 0.7
      : 0.4;

  const sourcePriceScore = {
    amazon: 0.95,
    ebay_bin: 0.85,
    ebay_auction: 0.6,
    other: 0.5,
  }[inputs.sourcePriceType];

  // Weighted combination
  const weighted =
    matchScore * 0.40 +
    sampleScore * 0.30 +
    freshnessScore * 0.15 +
    sourcePriceScore * 0.10 +
    inputs.categoryFamiliarity * 0.05;

  // Clamp to [0, 1]
  return Math.min(1, Math.max(0, weighted));
}
```

The weights reflect what we've found to be most predictive of recommendation quality:
- Listing match quality is the single most important factor (40%)
- Sample size is second (30%) — pricing from sparse data is inherently unreliable
- Freshness (15%), source price reliability (10%), and category familiarity (5%) round out the score

## Calibration

The algorithm is parameterised — the weights and thresholds are configurable. We calibrated them by comparing confidence scores to actual arbitrage outcomes: did high-confidence scans actually produce profitable purchases?

The calibration process:
1. Export all scans where users added the product to their watchlist (signal that they intended to buy)
2. For users who completed a purchase and gave feedback (the "was this profitable?" prompt), correlate confidence score with reported profitability
3. Adjust weights to maximise the correlation between score and outcome

After two iterations of calibration, the data showed a reasonably strong correlation between confidence score and profitable outcome — but a pronounced bimodal distribution. Scores clustered above 0.8 (exact match, strong data) or below 0.4 (inferred match, sparse data). The middle range (0.4-0.8) was almost empty.

This bimodal distribution was a signal: the algorithm was behaving more like a binary classifier than a continuous score. We redesigned the match scoring to be more granular — adding intermediate match quality levels for title similarity scores between 0.6 and 0.9 — and the middle range became more populated.

## What we show the user

Rather than showing the raw 0-1 score, we bucket it into four display levels:

| Score | Display | Colour |
|-------|---------|--------|
| 0.85+ | STRONG | Green |
| 0.65-0.84 | GOOD | Orange |
| 0.40-0.64 | MODERATE | Yellow |
| &lt;0.40 | LOW | Red |

The numeric score is in a tooltip; the bucketed label is in the primary UI. Users quickly learn to look for STRONG and GOOD scans; LOW scans are flagged prominently as "proceed with caution."

The recommendation: don't buy on a LOW confidence scan unless you have additional information about the product that Scoutr doesn't have access to.

## Ongoing improvement

Confidence score calibration is a continuous process. As more purchase outcomes are reported, the correlation data becomes more reliable and the weights can be refined.

The next improvement planned: incorporating external market trend data (category growth/decline) into the categoryFamiliarity factor. A category that's declining in the resale market should reduce confidence even if we have good data for it.
