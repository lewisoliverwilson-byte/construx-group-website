The journal started as a discipline: write about what you build, maintain a record of decisions, think in public. We didn't plan it as a product channel. It became one.

Here's what writing publicly has actually produced for our products.

## The unexpected discovery loop

The posts that attract the most attention are rarely the ones we expect. We've written strategic overviews of each product that got polite interest. We've written detailed posts about specific technical problems — how we built semantic search, how we handle incident response — and those got shared into communities of people who care about exactly those problems.

The people who share those detailed technical posts are, almost without exception, the people in our target user profile. A post about building pgvector-powered search attracted engineers building AI applications. A post about Amazon FBA fee calculations attracted resellers.

This is a selection effect. The people who find and engage with a specific technical post are filtered by their interest in that topic. They're not general internet traffic — they're the people for whom the product exists.

## Ideas from comments and responses

Four product features trace directly to journal post responses.

When we published "Scoutr's batch scanning mechanism", someone emailed asking whether we could export results directly to Google Sheets. We hadn't considered it — our users were already handling the export manually. The email was from someone who was doing exactly this workflow daily. We built the export. It became one of the most-used Scoutr features.

When we published "what The Marqet's categories look like", someone noted that "consulting and advisory" was missing as a professional category. We had 27 listings that could fall into it. We restructured the taxonomy.

Writing publicly surfaces the gap between what you built and what users actually need. Not from surveys — from the people who self-select into reading detailed posts about the problem you're solving.

## Writing as a forcing function for clarity

The posts that are hardest to write are the ones where we don't fully understand what we built.

This sounds obvious, but it's a useful diagnostic. When we shipped The Hyve's context pipeline and tried to write about it, we couldn't produce a clear post. The mechanism was right; the reason it was right wasn't clear to us. We spent three days writing drafts, discarded two of them, and in the process forced ourselves to articulate what the pipeline was actually for.

The final post was much shorter than the drafts. The product was also clearer afterwards — the writing process revealed that we were solving two different problems with one mechanism, and that we should separate them.

Writing forces specificity. Specificity reveals ambiguity. Revealing ambiguity is the first step in resolving it.

## The compounding effect

A post published in April is still being discovered in July. Some of our earliest dispatches still generate search traffic. The journal compounds in a way that most product channels don't.

This has two practical implications. First, the total value of a post is not its initial traffic — it's the sum of all the traffic it generates over time. A post that gets 20 readers in month one and 5 readers per month ongoing is worth more than a post that gets 50 readers in week one and is never found again.

Second, the archive becomes a product asset. A user evaluating Scoutr who reads 5 posts about how it was built, what problems it solves, and how we think about the market leaves that reading with a different level of trust than a user who only sees the landing page. The journal is the long-form argument for why the product exists.

## What we'd tell ourselves at the start

Start writing earlier than feels comfortable. The first posts are awkward; publish them anyway. The discipline of writing regularly compounds into a body of work with properties that no single post has.

Write about specific problems, not general overviews. "Here's how Scoutr works" attracts less of the right audience than "here's how we calculate Amazon FBA fees with multiple fulfillment scenarios."

Specificity is the filter. The more specific the post, the more it filters for people who actually care about that specific thing.
