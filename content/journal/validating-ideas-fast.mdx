Every venture we've built went through a validation process before we committed serious time to it. Not months of market research and business plans — 48 hours of structured work that gives us enough signal to decide whether to proceed.

The 48-hour number isn't magic. It's a constraint that forces prioritisation. You can't do everything in 48 hours, so you have to do the things that matter.

## What we're trying to find out

Before spending a day on validation, you need to be clear on what you're trying to learn. Validation isn't "is this a good idea?" — that question is too vague to answer in 48 hours. The specific questions we try to answer:

1. **Does someone need this?** Is there evidence of the problem existing in the world — not just our assumption that it exists?
2. **Is the economics viable?** Can we price it in a way that covers our costs and makes the business work?
3. **Can we build it with our current capabilities?** Not "could anyone build this" but "can we specifically build this with what we know and what we have?"
4. **Is AI genuinely the right tool?** Or are we reaching for AI because it's interesting, when a simpler approach would work better?

These four questions give us a framework for the 48 hours. We're not trying to answer everything — we're trying to get enough signal on these specific questions.

## The first 12 hours: evidence of the problem

We don't build surveys. We look for existing evidence that people have the problem.

The places we look:
- Reddit threads where people describe the problem in their own words (search for the pain, not the solution)
- Reviews of existing products that mention the limitation we're planning to solve
- Forum discussions, Facebook groups, Discord servers where the target user hangs out
- Job listings that describe roles we could automate or augment
- Products that partially solve the problem but have obvious gaps

For Scoutr, the signal was clear in reseller communities: people describing hours spent manually calculating margins across platforms, the mistakes they made, the products they missed because the calculation took too long. The problem existed and was felt acutely.

The output of this phase: a 10-15 line document of the best evidence we found, with source links. If we can't fill this document, that's information too.

## Hours 12-24: economics

Can the business work? We do a rough unit economics sketch.

The key numbers:
- What would someone pay for this? (find comparable products, their pricing, user reactions to that pricing)
- What would it cost to run? (Claude API costs per user session, hosting, rough estimated operational overhead)
- What conversion and retention would make this viable? (work backwards from what we need to reach break-even)

We don't build spreadsheets. We do the mental arithmetic and write down the key numbers. The goal is to identify any obvious economics problem — a product that would cost more to deliver than users would pay, or a market so price-sensitive that margin would be impossible.

For The Marqet, the economics check revealed something unexpected: the cost to create each listing (research + prompt engineering + writing) was higher than we'd estimated, which meant our initial price point wouldn't work. We adjusted the price before building anything.

## Hours 24-36: can we build it?

This is where we think about the technical reality. Not "could this be built" — of course it could, with enough time and money. The question is whether we specifically can build a good version of it.

We look for:
- Whether the core capability we need exists and is accessible (Claude API for the AI parts, available data sources for any data requirements, existing libraries for any complex functionality)
- Whether any part of the build is genuinely novel — something we'd have to figure out rather than implement
- Whether the quality bar for the first version is something we can reach

The conversation at the end of this phase is honest about our capability ceiling. If the build requires something neither of us has done before, the timeline and risk profile change significantly.

## Hours 36-48: the AI question

AI-first doesn't mean AI for everything. The last phase is asking whether AI is genuinely the right approach.

The failure mode we're checking for: using AI where a simpler deterministic approach would be faster, cheaper, and more reliable. A lot of "AI products" would be better as well-written regular software.

For each use of AI in the product, we ask:
- **Is the input variable?** (If not, a lookup table is probably better than an LLM)
- **Is the task complex enough to require reasoning?** (Simple classification doesn't need Claude)
- **Does the variable output add value, or create uncertainty?** (AI output variability is a feature when the task is open-ended; it's a problem when the answer should be deterministic)

If AI passes this check — the task is genuinely complex, the input is variable, and the variance in output adds rather than subtracts value — we proceed with confidence.

## The decision meeting

At the end of 48 hours, we have:
- Evidence file (10-15 lines of problem evidence)
- Unit economics sketch (key numbers, break-even calculation)
- Build assessment (what we can and can't do, how long)
- AI rationale (why AI specifically)

We sit with these and make a binary decision: proceed to a 2-week prototype, or not.

"Not" doesn't mean the idea is bad. It means we didn't find enough signal in 48 hours to justify the next investment. Sometimes we return to ideas after the market has matured or our capabilities have grown.

"Proceed" means we think the problem is real, the economics are plausible, we can build it, and AI is the right approach. It doesn't mean we're certain — it means we're willing to bet 2 weeks to find out more.

## What this process doesn't do

It doesn't validate demand. Evidence of a problem is not evidence that people will pay for your solution. The 48-hour process reduces the risk of building something for a problem that doesn't exist; it doesn't tell you whether your specific solution will find buyers.

That's what the prototype period is for.
