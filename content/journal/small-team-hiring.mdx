Two people. Three products. No employees.

This is not how most startup advice says you should operate. The advice is: hire fast, build the team, capture the market before competitors do. Headcount is leverage.

We've spent six months doing the opposite. Here's why.

## What AI changes about headcount

The baseline assumption behind "hire fast" advice is that human capital is the primary constraint on growth. You need more people to do more things.

AI changes this. Not completely — there are things AI can't do. But the category of work that previously required a human and now can be done by an AI-plus-human is large and expanding.

Six months ago, running three products with two people would have required either extremely narrow product scope, serious operational shortcuts, or burning out. We've shipped 53 journal posts, 167 marketplace listings, and meaningful product development across three products — with two people.

The AI layer handles: first drafts of content, code review, research synthesis, documentation, support draft responses, and significant portions of product analysis. We handle: the judgment calls, the strategy, the parts that require understanding the whole context.

The result: our effective output is closer to a 4-5 person team than a 2-person team. The marginal case for headcount is weaker when the marginal cost of AI assistance is low.

## Where the calculation changes

There are categories of work where human headcount still beats AI assistance decisively:

**Sales.** AI can draft outreach, but genuine sales relationships — especially enterprise ones — require a human who is curious about the customer's actual situation and can adapt in real time. If we were pursuing enterprise deals aggressively, we'd need a salesperson.

**Customer success at scale.** We currently handle support personally. This doesn't scale past a certain volume. An AI can draft responses, but a human needs to review them and handle escalations. Beyond some user count, we'll need dedicated support capacity.

**Technical depth we don't have.** There are engineering decisions where we need expertise we haven't built — database architecture at scale, specific compliance requirements, particular security domains. Hiring someone with deep expertise in a specific area where we have gaps is a different calculation than hiring for general capacity.

**Operational functions we've deliberately deferred.** We don't have a finance function, a legal function, or an HR function. For now, this works because we're small and simple. As we grow, each of these becomes a constraint.

## The right hire

If we were to make our first hire, we've been deliberate about what it would be.

It wouldn't be a developer. We can build. Adding a third developer would add communication overhead (more coordination, more code review, more architectural decisions to make jointly) without a proportionate improvement in output, given how much AI already handles.

It would be someone who makes the commercial side work better: a person who can own the customer relationship, understand what users need, translate that into product direction, and make sure the things we build reach the people who need them. The commercial gap in our operation is real. AI doesn't fill it.

The timing: when the revenue from one product is sufficient to support a competitive salary without creating financial pressure, and when the commercial constraint is visibly limiting growth. Not before.

## The coordination cost of early hires

There's a hidden cost to early-stage hiring that the "hire fast" advice understates: coordination.

Two founders with shared context can make decisions in 10 minutes that would take three people in a meeting 45 minutes. Adding a third person changes the decision-making dynamics before the person has enough context to contribute fully.

The coordination cost is front-loaded. The value of a hire is back-loaded. The early period — where you're paying coordination costs without the full value of the hire — is where many early hires fail.

We've chosen to defer this cost until the product and business are stable enough that a new person can be onboarded into something clear, rather than something still being figured out.

## The honest version

Two people is not infinitely sustainable. We know this. At some point, the commercial side will need more capacity than two founders can give it while also building products. At some point, the support volume will exceed what we can personally handle.

The question isn't whether to hire, but when and for what.

Our answer: when the gap is specific, the work is clear, and the revenue is there to make the hire without financial strain. Not earlier.

The companies that hire too early spend capital they don't have on people they can't fully utilise yet. The companies that hire too late leave growth on the table.

We're trying to thread the needle: stay small enough to move fast, hire specifically enough to fill real gaps, and avoid premature scaling that creates problems we don't have yet.
