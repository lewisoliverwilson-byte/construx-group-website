We get asked about hiring regularly. The products are live, the user counts are growing, and there's more to build than two people can build quickly.

The natural answer is: hire someone. The actual answer is more complicated.

## The two-person advantage

Two people who trust each other and have complementary skills can operate with almost no coordination overhead. There's no pull request waiting for a third reviewer, no alignment meeting to check if the strategy changed, no onboarding delay when we need to pivot.

Every conversation we have about the products is a strategy conversation. Every decision is made at the table with both parties. The feedback loop from observation to decision to implementation is measured in hours, not weeks.

This is an advantage that's genuinely hard to maintain as you scale. Adding a third person changes the group dynamics in ways that are irreversible. The two-person team isn't a stage to graduate from; it's a mode of operation with real capabilities that we'd lose.

## The two-person constraint

Two people can't do everything. There's more we want to build than we have time to build. Features wait. Marketing is under-invested. Customer support is reactive. Technical debt accumulates.

The constraint is real. The question is whether hiring solves it better than the alternatives.

## The alternatives to hiring

**Better tooling and automation.** We've automated significant portions of our repetitive work: deployments are fully automated, monitoring is automated, common support responses are templated and handled by AI draft. The marginal productivity gain from better tooling is often underestimated against the productivity cost of adding headcount.

**Doing less.** Not everything that could be built should be built right now. Feature prioritisation is a forcing function for clarity about what actually matters. A two-person team forces rigorous prioritisation that a five-person team can afford to skip.

**Slower growth.** Growing the product more slowly — accepting that some features wait — preserves the operating mode that makes the team effective. Fast growth with the wrong team composition produces worse outcomes than slow growth with the right one.

**Contract work for specific skills.** For work that's genuinely outside our skills (certain design work, specific infrastructure configuration), contract work is available. This is not the same as hiring — it's targeted capability acquisition without ongoing headcount cost.

## What would make us hire

**A forcing function.** If one of the ventures reaches a scale where the technical debt and support burden genuinely requires more people to serve users safely, we'd hire. We're not there.

**A specific irreplaceable capability.** If there's something we want to build that neither of us can learn adequately — a specific domain expertise, a capability that would take too long to develop — hiring is the right answer. This is distinct from work that's merely outside our current skill level.

**A specific person.** The right hiring decision often starts with a person, not a role. If we encounter someone who would make the company significantly better, the conversation about whether to hire becomes concrete.

## The honest uncertainty

We don't have a clean answer to when we'll hire. The products are growing; at some point the constraint of two people will become the binding constraint on growth rather than one factor among many. When that happens, hiring will be the right decision.

We're not there yet. And the instinct to hire because growth is happening — rather than because headcount is genuinely the binding constraint — produces a bigger team without proportionally better outcomes.

Two people who are productive, aligned, and moving fast are often more effective than five people with the overhead of coordination, onboarding, and alignment. We'll hire when the evidence points to hiring, not when the conventional wisdom says we should.
